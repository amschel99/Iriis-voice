## The lesson
**A different animal (pp. 182-184).** Before 2008, many families copied the **endowment model** used by Yale, Harvard and Princeton. After 2008 they realised they don't have to invest like institutions. The differences:
1. **A longer horizon**: multiple generations, not a quarter or one lifetime. That allows more freedom in asset allocation if liquidity needs are low.
2. **Patience**: they're after long-term appreciation, not a short-term exit value. One study found about **20% of UHNW assets** in hedge funds and private equity, locked up for long periods with low turnover. Family offices hold more alternatives and direct deals than most investors.
3. **Different risk tolerance.**
4. **No rigid mandates.** Institutions must sell any bond downgraded below investment grade. A family can buy into those dislocations.
5. **Less publicity pressure** than CalPERS or Yale, whose annual scrutiny pulls them toward the short term.
6. **Lighter governance requirements.**

An institutional mandate solves for one objective with minimum standard deviation. A family solves for **many objectives**, using broader risk measures such as maximum drawdown, loss probability and up/down capture.

Chris Battifarano (CIO, Palm Equity) has three points. Families worry about permanent loss of capital and shortfalls, not tracking error. They can back managers early in their life cycle, and the evidence suggests early-stage managers often do better: the principals are more involved and the asset base is small. And investing should be like a made-to-order meal at a small family restaurant, not chain fast food. **Families write their own rules.**

**Goals-based allocation (pp. 184-185).** Modern Portfolio Theory (the efficient frontier) maximises return for a given risk. Goals-based investing instead matches each goal to four principles: **liquidity, income, wealth preservation and growth**. Jack Parham's **pyramid** has three tiers, with "pain of loss" highest at the bottom:
1. **Needs and obligations** (base): lowest risk, highest confidence.
2. **Priorities and opportunities.**
3. **Desires and aspirations** (top): these can take more risk.

**The investment process (pp. 185-191).**
- **Investment review (p. 186).** What's owned and how it's titled. Active or passive? Concentration in real estate or the business? Who decides? How were goals monitored and governed? Has the family outgrown its strategy? Which metrics matter? How does the family see risk vs reward? How much is driven by taxes?
- **Investment philosophy (pp. 186-188).** The guiding values and beliefs, which keep evolving. Styles include **value**, **fundamental** (strong earnings), **growth**, **socially responsible**, the **triple bottom line** (John Elkington: profit, people, planet), **impact investing**, **technical** (patterns in past trading) and **contrarian**. Martin Whittaker of Sonen Capital sees impact investing, about 15 years old, growing fast with Gen X and Millennials who don't want to separate their philanthropy from their investing. Don't mix strategies that make contradictory market assumptions (note 24). The philosophy can also carry the founder's stories and faith.
- **Objectives (p. 188).** Functional goals: amounts for heirs or charity, return targets, growth, preservation of capital and purchasing power, income, absolute return. The split among families: **63% balanced**, **13% growth**, **3% aggressive growth**, **19% preservation**. Secondary goals: minimise expenses, estate efficiency, high-quality advisers, transparency, better family decisions, social impact.
- **Investment policy statement (pp. 188-190).** A quasi-legal mandate. Jean Brunel compares it to a pilot's **flight plan**: the destination, the route and the intermediate targets. It holds you accountable in the short term without becoming momentum-driven. Benefits: clear expectations, guidance for staff, a record of decisions, and it evolves over time. Typical components:
  - goals; philosophy; structures; time horizon; return goals;
  - rebalancing; risk profile and definition; risk tolerance; liquidity;
  - stock distribution policy; restrictions; eligible instruments; asset classes; acknowledged biases; preferred vehicles;
  - strategic allocation and allowed deviations; instrument guidelines;
  - taxes; performance review; fiduciary process; governance; risks; allocation review;
  - the investment council's role; conflicts of interest; custody and record keeping.
- **Investment advisory committee (pp. 190-191).** Family, outside professionals or both, acting like a board. It advises, oversees, challenges assumptions and recommends fixes. Cautions: set qualification criteria; be clear how much discretion the adviser has (especially with an outsourced CIO); and respond fast enough to catch short-term dislocations.
- **Strategic vs tactical allocation (pp. 191-193).** Strategic is the long-term mix, built from goals, liquidity, diversification, risk tolerance and capital market assumptions. Test tolerance with the question "how would you feel losing 10%, 20%, 30%?" Tactical is the day-to-day tilts of **6 to 18 months** to seize an opportunity or dodge a risk (for example, overweighting equities or underweighting high yield).
- **Manager selection and surveillance (pp. 192-193).** Front and back office due diligence; the manager's niche, philosophy, risk controls and the character of the decision makers; consistency across cycles; alpha; clean compliance. Lisa Gray: benchmark against a market index (better, a custom one) *and* against achievement of the family's goals.

**The CIO (pp. 193-196).** Reports to the family leaders, the CEO or the board, and works with the advisory committee. Mostly focused on preservation ("stay rich, not get rich"). Five functions:
1. Understand the underlying assets and their tax, legal, risk and liquidity limits, and translate the family's objective into strategy. "Family" grows more complex across generations.
2. Be the **majordomo**: strategic and tactical, acting for the family while staying close to them, through a growing web of trusts, illiquids, real estate, direct deals and private equity.
3. Organise the **process**: decision governance, manager hiring and monitoring, the IPS, constraints and benchmarks.
4. **Liaise** between the markets and the family: connections to top managers, and allocations shaped by capital market assumptions.
5. **Mitigate risk**: test every assumption; know managers' clearing firms, lawyers, accountants and prime brokers; set and monitor risk metrics.

**The outsourced CIO (pp. 196-198).** **47%** of family offices outsource the CIO role. Robert Casey (Family Wealth Alliance) describes three forms: an **investment consultant** (policy, allocation, managers, reporting), an **asset manager or manager of managers**, and an **investment planner** (liquidity, tax overlay, integration). Tom Livergood calls it a "scalable pillar". Two drivers:
- a **talent gap**: top CIOs have moved to big firms;
- **scale**: a full in-house platform needs about **$500 million**.

After Dodd-Frank, **61%** of outsourced CIO firms saw no rise in private-client business, and **35%** had more conversations. Fees average **73 bps** (investment firms) vs **85 bps** (multi-family offices) under $10M; **53 vs 72** for $10-20M; **41 vs 44** for $50-100M; negotiable above $100M. Families with an established process, owners who want to stay hands-on, or large scale may keep the CIO in-house.

**The Walford case (pp. 198-201).** Michael Sr. sold the family food-store chain in the late 1990s and started a family office in 1997. He was bold, with **30%+ in alternatives** on 10 to 20 year horizons, modest in lifestyle, and believed you should earn all you can, give some to heirs and the rest to charity. He died in 2005. **$225M** after tax went in trust to three sons and **$200M** to the foundation, all run by **Howard**, the president he hired.
- Mike and Steve wanted trendy, nimble investing through a consultant. They had Howard move trust assets to opaque providers, and Howard worried about risk, diversification and compliance.
- Henry, a cardiac surgeon worth **$150M+** in his own right through patents, disliked the "spray and pray" approach of chasing last year's top managers. He withdrew, ran an anonymous RFP through a consultant, and chose an outsourced CIO. His four criteria: an unconflicted fee model; talent; access to first-class thinking and products; and proximity.

He acted early, before the split divided the family.

## Notes

- Risk measures: maximum drawdown, loss probability, up/down capture, not only standard deviation (p. 183).

## Try it
**Draft a one-page investment policy statement** using Brunel's flight plan:
1. Destination: your three top goals, placed on Parham's pyramid (needs, priorities, aspirations).
2. Philosophy: two sentences, including your style(s).
3. Horizon, return target and liquidity needs.
4. Risk: your maximum acceptable drawdown (10, 20 or 30%?) and how you'll measure it.
5. Strategic allocation, with permitted tactical ranges (6 to 18 month tilts).
6. Governance: who decides, who checks, how often you review, and conflicts.
7. Benchmarks: one market index and one "goal achievement" measure (Lisa Gray).

## Questions
1. In the Walford case, put yourself in Howard's shoes when Mike and Steve ask him to move their trust assets to opaque providers. What would you do, using the IPS, the advisory committee and the CIO's risk role? And was Henry right to leave rather than fight?
2. Real world: many wealthy families, charities and feeder funds lost money with Bernard Madoff, whose fraud collapsed in December 2008. His steady returns were "too good", and his firm's books were checked by a tiny audit firm. Using this lesson's manager selection and CIO risk-mitigation duties (back office due diligence; knowing the clearing firm, auditors and prime broker), list the red flags a good family office CIO should have caught, and how.
3. Real world: Yale's endowment under David Swensen became famous for a heavy allocation to alternatives (private equity, hedge funds, real assets), and many families copied it before 2008. Using the "different animal" argument, explain where a family should copy the endowment model and where it should not.

## Answer key
1. Howard:
   - Point to the IPS: mandate, eligible vehicles, transparency and reporting requirements, conflicts (pp. 188-190).
   - Escalate to the investment advisory committee or board (pp. 190-191).
   - The CIO's risk function: test the providers' fees, reporting, custody and compliance (p. 196).
   - Document the decisions; respect the beneficiaries' authority while flagging the fiduciary concerns.
   - Henry: proactive, preserved family relations, and chose an outsourced CIO on clear criteria through a confidential RFP (pp. 200-201). Discuss the cost of splitting scale.
2. Madoff:
   - Unrealistically smooth returns.
   - Self-custody and self-clearing; a tiny, obscure auditor.
   - Lack of transparency on strategy.
   - Back office due diligence on custody, clearing firm, auditor and prime broker (pp. 192, 196).
   - Confirm balances directly with independent custodians (Chapter 3 advice, p. 61).
   - The book itself mentions Madoff prompting "who can I trust?" (p. 43).
   - Diversify across managers.
3. Yale model:
   - Copy: the long horizon and patience let a family hold illiquid alternatives and back early managers (pp. 182-183).
   - Don't copy blindly: families have multiple goals, taxes, lifestyle liquidity needs and trust constraints; institutions optimise one objective by standard deviation (pp. 172, 183).
   - Families learned this in 2008 (p. 182).
   - Use goals-based tiers (Parham) and keep liquidity for needs; the illiquid sleeve goes in "aspirations" (pp. 184-185).

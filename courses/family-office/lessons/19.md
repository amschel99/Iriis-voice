## The lesson
Today we start Chapter 10, on talent. Rosplock calls it under-discussed and one of the most important parts of building a family office that works. The money is managed by people. If you pick the wrong people, the best structure, investment policy and governance won't save you.

**Why family office talent is unusual.** Picture the job: someone with deep expertise in one or more wealth disciplines, who also has to connect with an 85-year-old "curmudgeon of a father", siblings in their 40s and 50s, and grandchildren in their teens and twenties. These professionals rarely follow a straight career path. They usually build expertise first in one area: investments, tax, law, estate planning, financial planning, accounting or wealth transfer. Many turn down bank or institutional jobs with bigger bonuses and perks because serving one family is what they want to do.

**How big the field is.** Nobody knows the exact number. FOX (Family Office Exchange) estimates more than 3,000 US families have family offices, and at least twice that number of offices sit inside private operating companies. Most are SFOs with 10 to 15 employees. Multi-family offices (MFOs) vary a lot in size:
- **Large** (Abbott Downing, Ascent, GenSpring Family Offices): can have more than 200 employees.
- **Medium** (Ballentine Partners, Threshold Group, Highmount Capital): roughly 25 to 50 staff.
- **Small** (Mirador Family Wealth Advisors, Chronicle Family Offices, Vogel Consulting): under 25.

Only 10 to 15% of family offices are MFOs. But their marketing has made the whole field more visible, and recruiters told the author that demand for dedicated family office professionals has risen since 2008. Families want people paid directly by them, acting as their agents and advocates. When an office is too small to justify a specialist, it outsources the role, for example by using a compliance consultant instead of hiring a compliance officer.

**No two org charts are alike.** A small office might be just a bookkeeper, a president and an administrator. A complex family with many trusts and partnerships needs more fiduciary capability: an estate planning attorney, a tax accountant, a risk advisor. An office focused on investments will have more people with CFA or CFP credentials. The structure follows how the wealth was created and set up, and what the family wants.

**The sample SFO organization chart (Figure 10.1).** The president/CEO sits at the top. Below are the CAO, CIO, CFO and COO, with an investment advisor, a wealth advisor, compliance and a bookkeeper under them. These are family office roles, separate from any roles in the family business.

- **President/CEO.** In a study, **7 in 10 family offices were led by a family member**: often the wealth creator or a second-generation owner who now leads "the new family enterprise", with the charisma to get the family investing together. When a family member leads, engagement tends to be higher. Non-family presidents usually came from the family business or worked as the family's wealth advisor, estate planner or investment consultant. Otherwise they are found through a long search, often with a specialist consultant. The typical president has 10+ years in the role, is about 50 to 60 years old, and reports to a board of directors. Their job is to run today's efficiency while keeping the multi-generational view. They are generalists, a "grasshopper" who can jump into any situation. Because the one constant in a family office is **change**, the president keeps the system **resilient**: flexible enough to grow and add structures without disrupting what already works. They are the family's chief steward and hold the office accountable. Opinions differ on whether this role must be a family member. Some families prefer an outside expert when the family lacks wealth-management depth.
- **CIO.** Sets the investment process, philosophy, standards, goals and objectives. A family member in only about a third of cases, but usually with the office about 10 years and with 20+ years of investment experience. Thinks both strategically and tactically, at the macro and micro level, leads due diligence and asset allocation, and is a strong student of risk. Most CIOs invest with a wealth-preservation lens: a respectable return after taxes, depreciation and amortization, guided by the investment policy statement. They also project wealth for trust and estate planning. This is the one role the author says should not be combined with others.
- **CFO.** Expert in tax, finance and accounting. Directs finance, manages liquidity across entities and produces consolidated reporting. Often the primary risk officer too. Links tax planning to the CIO's strategy. Owns the systems for tax strategy and compliance, investment tracking, estate and legal services, cash management, budgeting, the foundation, trusts and insurance. Often teaches the next generation about finance. Reports to the president.
- **CAO.** Monitors the family's operations and is responsible for all family employees, in the office and in the households: maintaining properties, managing property expenses, and often acting as philanthropic advisor. A jack-of-all-trades.
- **COO.** Oversees infrastructure, systems and processes: accounting oversight, compliance and regulation, sometimes senior counsel reviewing contracts, reporting and reconciliations. Focused on data: who accesses it, how it is stored securely, and operational risk.
- **Investment advisor.** Part technician, part relationship manager (CFA, CIMA or CIC). Turns the CIO's approach into each family's allocation, risk profile, IPS, liquidity and tax needs.
- **Tax accountant and bookkeeper.** The tax accountant gathers tax documents (K-1s) for the annual returns. The bookkeeper keeps the daily general ledger, reconciles accounts and tracks money in and out. Some offices outsource these roles; complex ones keep them in-house.
- **Wealth advisor.** Expert in tax, estate planning and accounting (CFP or ChFC). Advises on wealth transfer, fiduciary matters and multi-generational planning, works with the investment advisor, and liaises with insurance and risk specialists (life, asset protection, property and casualty).

The point to take away: in smaller offices one person may cover several of these roles, or the role is outsourced. What matters is that every function has an owner.

## Try it
Draw your own family office org chart, even if it's just you today.
1. List the functions: leadership, investments, finance and tax, administration and households, operations and compliance, advice to family members, bookkeeping.
2. Next to each, write who does it now: a family member, an employee, an outside firm, or nobody.
3. Mark any role combined with another. Is the CIO role sitting alone, as the author advises?
4. For any function marked "nobody", write what could go wrong (for example: no one reconciling accounts, so a fraud goes unnoticed) and whether you'd hire for it or outsource it.

## Questions
1. The author says 7 in 10 family offices are led by a family member, yet some families prefer a non-family expert as president. Using the book's description of the president's job (resilience, stewardship, the generalist "grasshopper"), when would you choose a family member and when an outsider?
2. Real world: Bill Gates' private investment office, Cascade Investment, has long been run by a non-family investment chief, Michael Larson, hired in the 1990s, while Gates himself built Microsoft. Map Cascade onto the book's roles. Which roles does a founder-led investment office need most, and why might Gates have wanted a dedicated CIO rather than a family member?
3. Real world: John D. Rockefeller Sr. started the family office in 1882 in what the book calls one room; by 2002 it filled three floors of 30 Rockefeller Plaza, with about 145 employees serving the family and around 200 non-family clients (the book's Chapter 1 figures, p. 12). As it grew from one room to three floors, which roles from the sample org chart would have had to appear, and in what order?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Family member: when the family has the expertise and appetite, wants higher engagement and stewardship, and the founder or a second-generation owner treats the office as the "new family enterprise" (pp. 235–236).
- Outsider: when the family lacks wealth-management depth, or wants objectivity and experience from other offices (p. 237).
- Either way: the president must be a generalist, keep the system resilient through constant change, and report to and be held accountable by a board (pp. 236–237).
- Bonus: notes that the CIO role is the one not to combine (p. 237).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Cascade is an investment-focused office, so the CIO role dominates (pp. 237–238); also needs a CFO for liquidity and tax and a COO for systems and data security.
- A dedicated CIO brings 20+ years of experience, a disciplined process and IPS, and lets the founder stay focused elsewhere.
- Links to the book: in only about a third of offices is the CIO a family member; investment talent is costly and specialized (p. 237).
- Avoids inventing facts about Cascade beyond what's stated.
3. Strong answer covers:
- Early: a president-like leader and bookkeeper or accountant (the "keeper and executor" role, Chapter 1).
- Then CFO/tax as entities, trusts and filings multiplied; CIO as the investment pool grew; COO and compliance once serving outside clients (regulation).
- CAO for households and properties; wealth advisors as more family members and branches needed service.
- Ties growth to complexity: structure follows how the wealth is set up and the family's goals (p. 235).

## The lesson
Chapter 6 is about the first serious piece of work any good family office does: building a **baseline**, a full picture of the family and its wealth. It covers estate planning, tax, legal, accounting, insurance, financial, compliance and family background. Some offices do this in-house and others outsource it. (Investment baselines come in Chapter 8.) If you're with a single family office, this shows what it should gather. If you're joining a multi-family office, it's the documentation you should be ready to hand over. It also leads into setting short- and long-term objectives in a multigenerational plan.

**The blueprint analogy.** No builder starts a house without a blueprint showing access points, wiring, structural supports and exact dimensions. A family office can't give truly integrated advice without one either. The family blueprint maps **financial capital** (wealth and business), **family capital** (who the members are) and **intentions** (wealth transfer and philanthropy). It establishes the knowns and surfaces the unknowns, and discovering unknowns can reset priorities.

**Five things to know about baselining:**
1. It means gathering a huge amount of information reviewed by many experts, so collaboration among advisors is essential.
2. It's an art and a science, and it takes time. Accountants, attorneys, bankers and trust officers each touch the family. A lot of knowledge lives in the **overlaps** between advisors, and how well they work together makes the process efficient.
3. It's a **snapshot**. It needs updating, monitoring and periodic review, and open lines with all advisors.
4. It's **iterative**. The office aggregates, organises and integrates, while technical advisors update as life changes (a birth creates a new trust, a child reaches majority and a trust terminates).
5. It isn't only documents. **Qualitative insight** matters as much: how members like to receive information, their decision styles, whether the family is open or closed about money. Good advisors notice when a client's values or intentions conflict with the legal documents. They know what drives clients, what motivates them, their dreams, their fears, and what they most want to achieve with their wealth.

**The genogram.** The family's greatest asset is its human capital, even though the industry measures itself by assets under management or advisement. A genogram maps the family tree and the relationships in it, including complications from divorce, death, separation or tension. Programs to draw one are available online. It shows how the family puzzle fits together at the macro and micro level.

**The family background.** Capture the wealth-creation story, formally (interviews, a family video, a biography) or informally around the dinner table or at reunions. Hearing it first-hand anchors the family to its roots and values and helps advisors appreciate the journey. A deep understanding of each member also reveals the hard realities of wealth: tensions, resentment, regret or inadequacy, feelings about preferential treatment or entitlement, pressure to live up to a creator's success or living in their shadow, and wealth creators who become isolated from family and peers. These are hard to talk about but explain why a family behaves the way it does with its money.

**Trusted advisors.** Identify the key advisors, typically attorneys, accountants, business executives, board members, family business consultants, insurance brokers, concierge specialists, and therapists or psychologists. For each, record who they are, their main role, which family member they serve, and their contact in the family office.

**The estate plan review.** The estate plan (will, power of attorney, trust documents, health care power of attorney, living will and **ethical will**) is perhaps the most important material to gather. Plans must evolve with the person. *Henry* set up his estate plan at 25. By 45 he was married with three children and his business had produced more than $20 million of liquid wealth. His plan is out of date on both his net worth and his family. The office, with his estate attorney (or one in-house), updates it proactively, for example with a marital trust and trusts for the children, and records his current wishes for heirs. The office is not just a vault for documents. It keeps them current and educates and prepares beneficiaries, grantors and trustees for a smooth transfer (more in Chapter 12).

**Mapping legal entities.** One family interviewed for the book had **more than 150 legal entities** (partnerships, LLCs, trusts). Others had **300, 400 or 500+**. More entities doesn't mean better planning, but it does mean more administration, reporting, compliance and oversight. For each entity, document:
- Why was it created? When?
- What purpose does it serve?
- Who set it up (family, advisors, attorneys, other professionals)?
- Who is legally connected (shareholders, owners, beneficiaries, grantors, trustees, partners) and in what role?
- When or will it terminate?
- Where was it established?
- What are its tax effects?
- Are there foreseeable issues or concerns?
- What "triggers" would require a change or update?

For trusts, also note whether they're **testamentary** (a gift at death) or **inter vivos** (a gift during life), **revocable** or **irrevocable**, plus the distribution provisions, powers of appointment and total return provisions. Work closely with the family's estate planning counsel. They benefit too, because provisions or circumstances may have changed.

The sample trust summary (Table 6.1) is the **1985 Jones Irrevocable Trust**: created 2/3/85; Florida law; taxed as a trust; year end 12/31; investment authority with the trustee. Its intent is to transfer wealth to the children of Mary and Mike Jones, who set it up after selling part of their business. Mary and Mike are the primary contacts; the children get statements at majority. Unusual provision: only "natural children" are beneficiaries. It has total return provisions. Income is distributed at the corporate trustee's discretion; no principal is distributed until termination. Succession: always two individual trustees and one corporate trustee. A surviving trustee appoints a successor individual trustee, and the individual trustee can choose another corporate trustee. The corporate trustee may renounce its discretion to accumulate or distribute income. $10,000 annual emergency payments are allowed, and a decision by two trustees binds all.

The sample estate flow chart (Fig. 6.1) follows a $34,154,000 joint revocable trust. At Mike's death it splits into a survivor's trust ($17,077,000), a marital trust ($13,577,000) and a GST family trust ($3,500,000). At Mary's death estate taxes are paid (the chart shows $4,534,650 and $7,684,650) before assets pass to a GPOA family trust for descendants ($14,934,700) and a GST family trust ($7,000,000). The lesson: a chart like this shows everyone *where* taxes hit and *where* the money ends up.

**Major life events.** Families are always living through births, deaths, marriages, divorces, retirement, job changes and more. Some need urgent action: a member loses a job and needs income or health insurance, or a business closes after a fire and a capital call follows. Others change gradually, like declining health, and call for planning. Bonnie Brown Hartley and Gwendolyn Griffith stress being proactive, because the stress and damage of being blindsided can be greatly reduced with simple planning. Examples: anticipating care, companionship and home oversight after a fall, or holding enough cash reserves in case a member is incapacitated or moves in with family. Life events are also reminders to update the estate plan or gifting schedule. Checklist: birth, death, retirement, unemployment, promotion, marriage, divorce, separation, adoption, moving abroad, a change in lifestyle, buying or selling a home, car, boat, aircraft or business, joining a board, a health change, an accident, surgery. Knowing the client is the core of all wealth advising, and especially so with a family office.

## Try it
**Start your own baseline this week.**
1. Draw a genogram of three generations of your family. Mark marriages, divorces, deaths and any tense relationships.
2. Write one page of the wealth-creation story, or interview the person who built it.
3. List every trusted advisor: name, role, who they serve, and how to reach them.
4. List every legal entity you know of, and answer the ten questions for one of them.
5. Tick off which life events from the checklist are likely in the next two years, and write the one action each would trigger.

## Questions
1. Henry's estate plan, made at 25, no longer fits him at 45. Name three specific updates the office should drive, and explain why the chapter calls the office more than a "repository."
2. The musician Prince died in 2016 without a will, and his estate went through years of court proceedings among his siblings and heirs. Using the estate plan review and the baseline, list the documents and steps that would have prevented most of that, and the role a family office could have played.
3. Aretha Franklin died in 2018. Months later, handwritten wills were found in her home, which led to a court fight over which document was valid. What does this teach about keeping the baseline a living, iterative record, and which items from the entity and estate checklist would have mattered most?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
   - A marital trust, trusts for the three children, updated beneficiary intentions (pp. 123-124).
   - Revisit the will, POA, health care POA, living will, perhaps an ethical will (p. 123).
   - Reflect his net worth ($20M+), possibly structure his business holdings; map new entities (pp. 123-124).
   - More than a repository: it keeps documents current and educates and prepares beneficiaries, grantors and trustees (p. 124).
   - Life events (marriage, births) as triggers (p. 127).
2. Strong answer covers:
   - Basic estate plan documents: a will, trusts, POA, health care POA (p. 123).
   - A genogram and the family background, to know the heirs and the tensions (pp. 121-122).
   - A trusted advisor map; entity mapping for business assets and IP (pp. 123-124).
   - The office as coordinator and updater; proactive planning reduces the stress of a sudden event (pp. 126-127).
3. Strong answer covers:
   - The baseline is a snapshot and must be updated and reviewed; it's iterative (pp. 120-121).
   - The office should be the central, current repository; know where every original is (pp. 123-124, 140).
   - Record intentions and spot conflicts between values or wishes and the documents (p. 121).
   - Work with estate counsel so formal documents match the current wishes (p. 125).
   - Ethical will and communication with heirs reduce disputes.

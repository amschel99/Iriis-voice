## The lesson
Chapter 12 opens with Lao Tzu: "When I let go of what I am, I become what I might be." Whether wealth lasts depends heavily on mentoring, education and awareness for family members of every age. This morning covers the psychology of growing up wealthy and the hard conversation about inheritance. Tonight covers the practical skills and education plan.

**Immigrants and natives.** Parents who weren't born rich are "immigrants" to wealth; their children are "natives" (Jaffe and Grubman). Immigrants feel pride at giving their children an easier, safer life, with access to mentors, education, travel and networks. That head start can create a false sense of security that the children will succeed on their own.

**Individuation.** From early childhood to young adulthood, every person works out who they are: values, goals, limits, independence, what career and family they want. Wealth can enrich this or disable it. "The blessing of wealth can become a debilitating curse that thwarts ambition and poisons the spirit."

**The golden ghetto.** Jessie O'Neill (granddaughter of Charles Erwin Wilson, a former president of General Motors, and now a therapist specializing in the psychology of money) wrote _The Golden Ghetto_. A poor child may lack food and shelter. A child in a golden ghetto has every material thing but feels the same longing and loss, because human connection, love and belonging are missing.

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** (Figure 12.1, bottom to top):
1. Physiological (food, water, sleep)
2. Safety (body, resources, family, health, property)
3. Love and belonging
4. Esteem (confidence, achievement, respect)
5. Self-actualization (morality, creativity, problem solving)

People focus on the lowest unmet need. Wealthy children have the bottom two met to excess but may be badly "impoverished" at love, belonging, esteem and self-actualization. The resulting isolation can be very hard to overcome.

**Elsa's story.** Elsa, an inheritor in Manhattan, was raised to be modest and appreciative, a steward of wealth several generations old. She felt both pride and shame and hid her wealth from friends in a simple one-bedroom apartment. Her inheritance could cover her lifestyle, but it meant less to her than money she earned herself. Her supplemental payments from the family office made her feel dependent and resentful. When she finally earned enough to stop them, she felt free.

**Joanie Bronfman**, an inheritor and sociologist, studied inheritors and documented low self-worth and self-confidence, resentment and hostility. Healthy self-esteem and identity are the bedrock of good stewardship.

**Entitlitis.** Lee Hausner (_Children of Paradise_) describes child-centric homes where the family revolves around the kids and "everyone wins trophies". The alternative is a **family-centric** approach: children and parents have separate interests; parents encourage experiences that set children apart from them, success or failure; the family values **doing** over **having**; independence over dependence.

**Emerging adulthood.** Between 12 and 30, two big tasks are finding an authentic identity and learning to sustain intimate relationships. Jeffrey Arnett's "emerging adulthood" (18 to 26) is marked by identity exploration, instability (trying colleges, jobs, partners), self-focus, feeling in-between, and a sense of possibility. It is even stronger in affluent families. Sociologists Schnaiberg and Goldberg describe "incompletely-launched young adults". Their table compares living at home with living independently:
- Material comfort: high vs. low (strong negative to leave)
- Economic security: high vs. low (strong negative)
- Privacy: moderate vs. high (weak positive)
- Social freedom: moderate vs. low-to-high (mixed)
- Sexual freedom: moderate vs. high (weak positive)
- Emotional support: high vs. low-to-high (negative or none)

In a wealthy home, staying is even more attractive. Without a true identity, young people either rebel or conform. Eileen and Jon Gallo warn against giving adult children money for copying their parents' choices: it makes money a prize instead of a tool, and creates a "vicious cycle of hostile dependency": resentment, rebellion, bailout, repeat.

**What the research says families want.** Mindy Rosenthal (Institute for Private Investors): affluent parents want a culture of appreciation, modest pride, truth and stewardship. **100%** of 25- to 49-year-olds in a Morgan Stanley/Campden study said being a good steward matters. A 2011 Charles Schwab survey of teens found:
- 89% want to learn to invest
- 65% find money management interesting
- 60% call it a top priority
- 82% have learned about money from their parents
- 77% see their parents as money role models

Teach where the wealth came from and how big it is, then add complexity with age: investing, taxes, credit, mortgages, philanthropy. **73%** of parents aged 25 to 49 use philanthropy as a teaching tool, and families also use giving to work through intergenerational conflict.

**Telling the truth about inheritance.** People who know about their present and future wealth handle it better. Satisfaction with investment decisions was 92% for heirs in their forties and 75% in their thirties. But satisfaction with the family's **wealth transfer plan** was 84% for 40- to 49-year-olds and only **14%** for those in their thirties. Williams and Preisser (_Preparing Heirs_) attribute **60%** of failed wealth transfers to breakdowns in communication and trust. Ellen Perry's advice:
- **Model** your values.
- Teach that the money has **meaning**: how it was made, ancestors' stories, traditions.
- Give members a **voice** in decisions so they take responsibility.

Be honest and age-appropriate, covering both the opportunities and the difficulties.

In a 2006–2007 study of men and women, 63% said they discuss money openly and 55% had told their children about their inheritance. Women are more open: 63% vs. 47% have discussed their estate plan with their children, and 80% vs. 73% have taught their children responsibility with money. Minors usually don't see trust statements (the trustee does) until the age of majority, but kids notice how differently they live. Jamie Johnson, a Johnson & Johnson heir, made the film _Born Rich_ at 23, opening on the night he turned 21 and was set to inherit more than most people earn in a lifetime. Heirs he interviewed (Trumps, Vanderbilts, Newhouses, Bloombergs) described the taboo on talking about money, the difficulty of real friendships, and struggling to find an identity apart from the money. He spoke of fearing "voodoo inherited wealth".

**Aragon Trust Company case.** Before a family meeting in the western US, the office executive and trustee dreaded telling Michael, about to turn 18 and living simply in a commune in Taos as an artist who read Nietzsche and Plato, about his huge inheritance. They feared entitlement and that he wouldn't build a career. On a walk, he told the author he had known all along. He was humble and grateful. His real fear was letting the family down, and he wanted guidance without looking like he "just wanted the money". The silence had created anxiety on both sides. The next meeting built an education curriculum covering finance, heritage, values and beliefs.

## Try it
The "inheritance conversation" plan:
1. Pick one young person in your family (or picture your future child).
2. Write three things they should know by 18: where the wealth came from, roughly what to expect, and what is expected of them.
3. Write one family story that shows your values (Ellen Perry's "meaning").
4. Plan one decision you'll give them a real voice in this year (a donation, a budget, a trip).
5. Check yourself against Maslow: which of their higher needs (belonging, esteem, purpose) does your plan feed?

## Questions

2. Real world: Liesel Pritzker, an heir to the Pritzker (Hyatt) fortune, sued her father and other relatives in 2002, alleging that family trusts set up for her had been drained. The case was later settled, reportedly for about $900 million for her and her brother. Using this lesson (communication, transparency with heirs, the 60% statistic), what does the case teach about keeping beneficiaries in the dark?
3. Real world: Cornelius Vanderbilt was among the richest Americans of the 1800s, yet within a few generations much of the family fortune had been spent. How do the golden ghetto, entitlitis and incompletely-launched young adults help explain how such a fortune disappears?

## The lesson
This morning covered offices born inside a business and from sudden wealth. Tonight: the investment office, the accidental office, the business sale, and the Kessler family case, which is one of the most practical stories in the book.

**The investment office is not a family office (pp. 31–32).** Some first-generation wealth creators see their wealth as a *new business*. An investment office has a rigorous mandate to "grow wealth from wealth." It is purely a business pursuit, and the creator is usually involved day to day.
- **What it invests in:** real estate, private equity, direct deals, alternatives, stocks and bonds, and operating businesses.
- **Who runs it:** sophisticated investment professionals.
- **How it behaves:** opportunistic and flexible, within its risk tolerance, liquidity needs and mandate. It cares little about legacy or multi-generational planning.

Phil Strassler (Strassler LLC) calls the founders of such offices **"do-it-yourselfers" (DIY)** who love the business. They are often former investment managers, institutional investors, or venture capital and private-equity people who now have enough money to manage their own. It isn't unusual for one to own a hedge fund as an underlying asset. **The key difference is the role of family.** In an investment office the wealth story is disconnected from the family, and managing the wealth is kept independent of family dynamics by design.

**Family offices through the back door (pp. 32–33).** Many families never *intend* to build a family office. After a liquidity or transfer event they just solve immediate problems: taxes, accounting, reporting, investments. Without realizing it, they assemble the services of a family office.
- **"James" and "Mark":** James runs the back office and operations and is general counsel. Their father was a successful estate-planning attorney who set up family offices for his larger clients but never thought his own wealth needed one. After his sudden death, the brothers realized his financial legacy *required* one. Mark heads investments.
- **How it runs now:** as a *virtual* family office, with the brothers and staff in different US cities.

**The family business exit (pp. 33–34).** Selling the business is often a major catalyst, especially when "the tie that binds is not equity in the company, but familial bonds." A big inflow of cash can surface issues that "lay dormant under the veil of family harmony."
- **Hap Perry**, founder of GenSpring, calls it the **"magnifier effect"**: wealth amplifies whatever dynamics already exist, healthy or harmful. If the family isn't aligned on its values, conflict comes easily.
- **Jay Hughes** (*Family: The Compact among Generations*): "Families of affinity, not families of blood, will be those who flourish five generations into the future." When private stock no longer binds a family, its bonds are truly tested. But the same wealth also creates new ways to work together.

**The Kessler family case (pp. 34–39).**
- **The business:** Fel-Pro of Skokie, Illinois, the leading US maker of automotive gaskets. It had 3,000 employees in 11 US cities and six countries and about $500M in sales, and had been in the family nearly 80 years. Nine family members across three generations worked there. *Fortune* ranked it the fourth-best company to work for in America, and it was on *Working Mother*'s top-10 list for 12 years.
- **The first plan:** the three family branches went to FOX to learn how to separate the family office from the company's accounting staff.
- **The pivot:** while exploring this, the question of *selling* came up. A 10-month strategic review involved all 35 stockholders. The family agreed to proceed with a family office either way.
- **The decision:** opinion swung from 100% for keeping the business to 100% for selling, after seeing market changes such as more cars being built overseas. It was hard, because the dream was always to pass the business on.
- **Kessler's "two hats" question:** "When you're speaking now, are you speaking with your business hat on or your family (emotional) hat?" Ask what an outside consultant would advise, then what you'd like to do, and find the sweet spot.

**Four kinds of office from one family (pp. 36–37).** After the sale, each branch picked functions from FOX's lists, individually, by family and by branch, while thinking about generations 2–4.
1. **"Jones": a classic, full-service office.** A physical office, pooled money ("Do we want to pool? Advantages? Comfortable investing as a group?" yes to all three), LLCs per asset class, fee-based advisors instead of fees on assets, a hired CFO, and procedures and governance.
2. **"Smiths": a modified-classic office.** One part-time employee, a former Ernst & Young accountant, two days a week in a small rented office, handling accounting, investment coordination and business affairs. Family members work from home.
3. **Kessler's own: a virtual office.** It serves seven households across three generations. Kessler and his brother-in-law lived 30 miles apart, so each kept his own office, and they split tasks and pooled investments. Mrs. Kessler, the office manager, runs the family foundation, and a senior bookkeeper comes in two mornings a week.
4. **A multi-family office.** After several years the brother-in-law's family left to join one.

**Kessler's five recommendations, 15 years on (pp. 37–39):**
1. **Park it.** Don't rush to deploy money after a liquidity event. Be methodical, check references, interview clients who *left* a firm, and remember that unwinding investment relationships is slow.
2. **Take a clean-slate approach.** Re-examine every advisor: bankers, accountants, lawyers, estate planners, brokers, insurers. Do we still need them, and how has the relationship changed? Kessler moved from a full-service accounting firm to a boutique plus a senior bookkeeper. Be wary of loyalty: some family members followed a long-time employee into his new investment firm and later had to scale back.
3. **Get outside, objective advice if you're stuck** on family dynamics, unclear outcomes, costs, or what to do in-house versus outsource. Check the advisors for conflicts. You might even decide a family office isn't right for you.
4. **Form follows function.** Don't build "the Rolls Royce of family offices." Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves; a hybrid may be fine (Chapter 5).
5. **Flexibility and adaptability.** The only constant is change. Needs differ by generation: in-laws wanted paper and meetings, younger members want digital reports and Skype.

**Conclusion (pp. 39–40).** Paths differ: one generation's success, a sale after several generations, an office you only discover you have, or an investment office that keeps the family separate. *Time* matters too: a sudden event versus slow, gradual growth. The source and history of the wealth tie into the family's vision.

## Notes
- Investment office = "grow wealth from wealth"; opportunistic; little focus on legacy; family kept separate by design (pp. 31–32)
- Phil Strassler: investment-office founders are DIYers, often ex-investment, venture or private-equity professionals (p. 32)
- Back-door office: assembled by necessity; James and Mark run a virtual office (pp. 32–33)

- Fee-based advisors as an alternative to fees on assets under management (p. 36)

## Try it
**Run the Kessler exercise.** Write a list of possible family office functions (accounting, record keeping, investment management, tax, wealth-transfer planning, bill pay, foundation administration, reporting, governance). Have each household tick what it needs: must-have or nice-to-have. Then compare households. Do they point to one classic office, a modified-classic office, a virtual office, or some going to a multi-family office? Finish by answering Kessler's two-hats question for one big decision your family faces.

## Questions
1. Kessler's branches ended up with four different structures. What process did they use to decide, and which of his five lessons best explains why one branch later moved to a multi-family office?
2. Around 2010–2011, well-known hedge fund managers including Stanley Druckenmiller, George Soros and Carl Icahn returned outside investors' money and continued as private offices managing mainly their own and their families' wealth. Using Strassler's "DIY" description and the investment-office vs family-office distinction, how would you classify these offices, and what might they lack as the founders age?
3. Imagine a family that has just sold a business for $400 million and gets calls from six private banks the next week. Apply "Park It" and "Clean Slate": what should they do in the first six months, and what should they avoid?

## Answer key

- Branches reviewed lists of family office functions individually, by family and by branch, and thought about generations 2–4 (p. 36).
- Results: classic, modified-classic, virtual, and later a multi-family office (pp. 36–37).
- Lesson 5 (flexibility; needs evolve) and lesson 4 (form follows function) explain the move (p. 39).
- Strong answers mention that each household's needs and cost-to-scale differ.

2.
- They're investment offices: grow wealth from wealth, run day to day by DIY founders (pp. 31–32).
- Focused on assets, less on multi-generational planning or family cohesion (p. 32).
- As founders age, they risk missing succession, heirs' preparation and governance (link to Chapter 3's founder's office and Chapter 11).
- Strong answers keep the facts conservative: they returned outside capital around 2010–2011 and kept managing their own money.
- Bonus: link to Dodd-Frank (Chapters 3 and 7).

3.
- Park the money temporarily; be methodical; don't feel pressure to deploy (pp. 37–38).
- Check references, including clients who left; remember unwinding is slow (p. 38).
- Re-examine all existing advisors; watch for advisors who've been outgrown and for misplaced loyalty (p. 38).
- Get objective, unconflicted outside advice; consider whether a family office is even right (p. 38).
- Watch the magnifier effect and family alignment (p. 33).

## The lesson
Tonight finishes Chapter 6 with the money side of the baseline: the financial review, capital sufficiency analysis (with the Raymond family case) and the insurance review.

**The financial review.** Alongside the estate documents and entity map, gather the financial data: personal and business income tax returns, and statements from brokerage, pension, 401(k) and investment accounts. With the family's permission, bring in both the personal accountant and the business accountant. Then organise everything **by household** and enter income, expenses, taxes, investments and performance into financial modelling software. Such programs model income and expenses, run cash-flow analysis, project wealth accumulation and plan gifting schedules. The key is *customisable* software that lets the office change capital-market assumptions and model the family's own situation.

**Capital sufficiency analysis** answers two questions: do we have enough to fund our lifestyle, and what's left for heirs, charities or beneficiaries, now or at death? It usually uses a **Monte Carlo simulation**, which gives the probability of reaching goals under set conditions. Inputs: current net worth, monthly lifestyle spending, liens or obligations, and all income streams. Inflation and taxes are built in, but overrides are better. The output is the likelihood that spending and gifting goals can be met.

Used with a seasoned advisor, it is "tremendously powerful." It can bring peace of mind; show that a family must cut back, or that it has a surplus; engage beneficiaries about budgeting; show when someone living beyond their means will run out; give confidence for big purchases (a home, a car, a large gift); show how one decision ripples through; and clarify priorities, since goals with low odds often turn out to matter less.

**The Raymond family case.** Jessica and Hank Raymond have about **$45 million** invested and are confident it's enough, but they have looming commitments. Their goals, in order:
- Keep their lifestyle at about **$1.5 million a year**.
- Leave **$5 million to each child** ($15 million in total for three) and **$1 million to each grandchild** ($3 million), at death or during life.
- They hold equity in a struggling company. If it goes into receivership and the bank calls loans, they could owe creditors up to **$6 million within two years**.
- If all that works, fund their foundation with **$3 million** at death.

Assumptions: a 30-year horizon; 1,000 simulations; a total portfolio of about $48,000,000; total expenses (before tax) $2,208,273; tax rates of 35% ordinary income, 15% capital gains, 5% state; 25% annual turnover (75% of gains long-term); 2.6% of return as income and 6.0% as growth.

The four cases:
- **Case 1:** the company stock is worth $3.5M and survives, no $6M hit, no foundation gift, $18M (inflation-adjusted) to heirs.
- **Case 2:** as Case 1, plus the $3M foundation gift ($21M total transfer).
- **Case 3:** the stock is worthless, the $6M hit lands in year two, no foundation gift, $18M to heirs.
- **Case 4:** as Case 3, plus the $3M foundation gift.

Results:
- **Case 1:** 95.1% that assets last through retirement; 56.9% that the target inheritance passes to heirs.
- **Case 2:** 94.4% assets last; 49.3% for heirs plus foundation.
- **Case 3:** 79.4% assets last; about 31% for heirs (the text says 31.4%, the chart 31.5%).
- **Case 4:** 78.8% assets last; 23.9% for heirs plus foundation.

The lesson: the business failure and the decision about the foundation gift drive the outcome. Even "rich enough" families can have well under even odds of meeting their legacy goals. Results are snapshots, not guarantees.

**How to read the odds.** Christina Burroughs (Miller | Russell & Associates): capital sufficiency gives transparency on lifestyle, income, expenses and whether goals are reachable, and it helps clients prioritise competing goals. The advisor's rule of thumb:
- **85% or higher** confidence: likely achievable.
- **65-85%:** a very real chance of missing one or more goals.
- **Below 65%:** re-prioritise goals or assumptions.

It depends on the client, though. Burroughs had a client comfortable at a **60-70%** success rate, because he had shown he would cut spending sharply when needed, and he did. Her colleague Matt Walker: "The science is running the reports, the art is how to interpret the data and apply it to each family's unique situation." **Tip:** rerun the analysis after any significant change in assets or liabilities.

**The insurance review.** Many more offices outsource insurance advice (**49%**) than provide it in-house (**2%**). Insurance isn't most families' core business, so the office coordinates specialists, and insurance is a central piece of risk management. A Crystal & Co. study with FOX found family office executives lack confidence about insurance: "When you don't know what you don't know, that *scares* you." People value insurance most after a hard knock, offices struggle to engage families on it, and they often lack the resources to source, price and structure coverage.

How to approach it: start with the family's own concerns and any coverage gaps from changed circumstances. Then look at the whole programme, gathering every policy (an objective third-party advisor helps). Crystal & Co.'s **"What keeps you up at night?"** assessment ranks 13 concerns from highest to lowest risk:
1. Personal security (kidnapping, extortion, burglary and theft, home surveillance and invasion, computer hacking)
2. Lawsuits from outside board positions (e.g. non-profit boards with too little liability cover)
3. Employment practices lawsuits (discrimination, harassment, wrongful termination)
4. Liability from dependent children (young drivers, sports, social networking)
5. Medical travel risk (evacuation, access to proper care)
6. Identity theft, fraud, theft by personal staff
7. Damage to physical assets (fire, hurricane, earthquake, vandalism, flood, theft)
8. Damage or loss of value to collections (art, jewellery, silver, wine)
9. Premature death or disability (life, disability, long-term care)
10. Quality and sufficiency of insurance for the operating business or office
11. Owning, operating or chartering aircraft, watercraft or yachts
12. Hosting events at home or public venues (liquor liability, subcontractors)
13. Other

Jonathan Crystal, a third-generation owner of Crystal & Co. (which the book calls the largest privately held insurance brokerage in the US), who leads its Private Client Services group and was its CFO, notes that some families treat insurance as an administrative chore done under duress, while others build it into their wealth management for peace of mind. The key is a consistent, repeated process: **identify** the risks, **evaluate and mitigate** them, and **transfer** risk. Map the family's full footprint (physical injury, property, safety and security, litigation, and business, personal, professional and financial risks).

The process: the office has a first conversation with an insurance advisor to set goals, risk tolerance and any group-purchasing opportunities; draws up an initial plan and timeline; gets family buy-in; the advisor meets the family to review property and liability exposure and lifestyle (travel, entertaining, collections, board seats); the advisor designs a risk management programme for **each household** (education on transferring risk, disaster planning, other mitigation); then the office briefs the family on implementation, roles and regular meetings.

Finally, risk tolerance differs. Some families resist the immediate cost and accept the consequences, even total loss. Others can't bear the fear of loss and gladly pay for comprehensive cover. The office must explore how each family perceives loss, its financial severity, and its emotional impact. The chapter's close: the baseline is like taking the family's pulse, a dynamic central repository that guides both strategy and the office's direction.

## Try it
**Two exercises.**
1. *Back-of-envelope capital sufficiency:* list your yearly lifestyle spending, obligations, income streams and investable assets. Then list your legacy goals in priority order. Ask: if one big liability hit (like the Raymonds' $6M), which goal would you drop first? Mark each goal "must have" (aim for 85%+ confidence) or "nice to have."
2. *"What keeps you up at night?":* rank the 13 concerns from 1 (highest risk) to 13 for your own household. For your top three, write how you'd identify, mitigate and transfer each risk.

## Questions
1. Raymond family: why does adding the $3M foundation gift barely change the odds that their assets last (95.1% to 94.4%) but cut the inheritance odds a lot (56.9% to 49.3%)? If you were their advisor, what would you recommend, given the 85% and 65% bands?
2. In 1973 J. Paul Getty's grandson was kidnapped in Rome, and his grandfather, one of the world's richest men, at first refused to pay the ransom. How does this case map onto the first item of the "What keeps you up at night?" ranking, and what would a modern family office's insurance and security review include for a family like that?
3. Many families who drew regular income from Bernard Madoff's funds learned in 2008 that their "assets" didn't exist, and some had to rethink their lifestyle and gifts overnight. Using capital sufficiency analysis, explain why the chapter's tip to rerun the analysis after any significant change matters, and how you'd stress-test a family's plan against a single-manager or single-asset failure like the Raymonds' company.

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
   - Asset longevity is mostly driven by lifetime spending and the $6M risk; the foundation gift comes at death, so it eats into the residual estate for heirs rather than lifetime security (pp. 131-135).
   - Inheritance odds are below 65% in every case, so re-prioritise: e.g. reduce or restructure the heir targets, give during life, lower spending, or settle the foundation amount (p. 132).
   - The business failure is the biggest driver (Cases 3-4: ~79% assets, 31% or 24% legacy) (pp. 133-135).
   - Knowing the client: a client willing to cut spending might accept lower odds (p. 132); rerun after any change (p. 132).
2. Strong answer covers:
   - Personal security risk (kidnapping, extortion) is the top item on the ranking (p. 137).
   - Process: identify, evaluate and mitigate, transfer (Crystal, p. 138); a household-specific programme; review of lifestyle and travel (p. 138).
   - Mitigation: security planning, travel protocols, disaster or crisis plans; transfer via specialist cover such as kidnap and ransom insurance (an inference; the book discusses risk transfer generally).
   - Risk tolerance and the family's emotional response to loss (pp. 138-139).
3. Strong answer covers:
   - Capital sufficiency needs accurate inputs; a large asset vanishing changes everything (pp. 128-129); rerun after significant changes (p. 132).
   - Stress-test like Cases 3-4: model losing the largest position or manager (pp. 131, 133-135).
   - Keep "must have" goals (lifestyle) at 85%+ confidence; put legacy goals at greater risk (p. 132).
   - Link to verifying managers and avoiding concentration (chs. 3, 5).

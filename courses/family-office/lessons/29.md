## The lesson
The last chapter takes the family office on a world tour. Once seen as a Western idea, it is now emerging everywhere, shaped by each region's culture, politics and economy. The method: an economic snapshot of each region's ultra-high-net-worth (UHNW) population, then interviews with professionals who run family offices there (p. 358). Tonight: Asia and North America.

**Asia: the numbers** (p. 358). The 2008 crisis didn't derail Asia; it slowed the growth. Regional GDP dipped in 2011 but is expected to stabilize and rise, positioning Asia as a source of stability. Wealth-X counts **44,505 UHNW individuals worth $6.6 trillion**, up 3.8% since 2012. China slowed, but Japan, Hong Kong and Indonesia grew. In Japan, "Abenomics" (PM Shinzo Abe's policies) helped the Nikkei 225 rise over 50% in yen terms and over 21% in dollars. China's economy grew 7.9%, its slowest in 13 years (7.5% forecast for 2013), yet billionaires are over 25% of China's UHNW population while those with $500M or less shrank. India faced high food costs, a weak currency and public debt; its UHNW population grew 1.6%, and 16% of its UHNW individuals are women, with a combined $95 billion.

**Asia: culture and the family office** (pp. 359-362). Laurent Roux says Asian family offices have long existed in some form: rooted in family business, driven to grow and preserve wealth, and facing the same pitfalls as the West. Post-war wealth creation and generational transitions make Asia a premier region for family office growth, and each culture manages family and money differently.
- **Chinese families** are shaped by entrepreneurship, Confucianism, harmony, autocratic rule, filial piety, self-reliance, pragmatism, privacy and avoiding loss of face. Multiple wives can stretch a family's child-bearing span to 50-60 years instead of 20-30, multiplying relationships and wealth-transfer puzzles. Imagine a 45-year-old eldest son, working his way up the business, learning his father will split it with a 5-year-old half-sister from a second wife. Decisions centralize in the entrepreneur, increasingly balancing sons and daughters; family and business are commingled, which helps efficiency but breeds conflicts at the ownership level.
- **A next-gen trend:** some second-generation heirs would rather enjoy than preserve wealth. Combine that with the single-child policy and its generation of single males, which Friedman calls "an interesting cocktail for generational wealth planners."
- **Emigrant Chinese** built businesses across Asia and see the world as their investment field, but qualified family office staff are scarce. Wendy Craft notes a global shortage, with about a third of experienced executives aged 60 or older. Many heirs study in the U.S. and return with Western ideas about wealth and family offices.
- **Tax and banking:** many Asian countries have no estate or gift taxes and tax income territorially, so families hold assets in many jurisdictions through private banks. Banking has been the norm because it's centralized and confidential. Families deliberately spread assets across institutions so no single institution, or even family member, knows the full scale. Rapid legal and financial change brings opportunity and risk, and newly wealthy families now see the need for a wealth-transfer plan.
- **Hubs:** experts estimate 25-50 family offices in Hong Kong, serving Chinese, Indian and Western families, mainly with asset allocation and investment management, with risk management growing. Hong Kong is an international center of banks, law and accounting firms; offices there are separating business from wealth management and doing multi-jurisdictional planning. Singapore attracts families from Southeast Asia, Indonesia, Australia, Europe and the Middle East.
- **Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand and the Philippines** have fewer complex family offices; corporate/business offices that handle both business and wealth are the norm. Decisions stay inside the family; confidentiality and security matter; staff turnover is low out of duty and loyalty.
- **Indian families** value autocratic rule, family loyalty, self-sufficiency, practicality and privacy. Family matters aren't handed to non-family staff; business and family are intertwined, so wealth preservation and business growth compete. Family offices exist in Singapore and Hong Kong, often as in-house investment offices.
- **MFOs in Asia:** close Chinese groups eat, travel and invest together, and refusing an idea can mean losing face. Many self-styled MFOs are really private banks without holistic advice. There is room for true MFOs with open architecture, co-investing and stewardship programs.

**North America: the numbers** (pp. 362-363). The U.S. economy sets the stage for global markets. Modest growth in 2012 gave way to records in May 2013, with the S&P 500 breaking 1,600 and the Dow 15,000, thanks to near-zero rates and quantitative easing, though consumers remained tentative. The U.S. is the largest UHNW country: UHNW wealth up 9% since 2012 and the population growing 7.9%. Its **515 billionaires** are 23% of the ultra-affluent. U.S. UHNW women are 13% of the country's UHNW individuals and hold $1.2 trillion, more than women in any other country. California has the most (up 14.7%), fed by Silicon Valley, San Francisco, Los Angeles, San Jose and Santa Barbara. The fastest growth was Massachusetts (35%), Indiana (28.8%) and North Dakota (27.3%); New York City has the most UHNW people, over 8,000. Canada's economy slowed in 2012 on household debt; its UHNW people are 7% of North America's, worth $595 billion versus the U.S. $9,085 billion, and the proportion of Canadian UHNW women is four times lower than in the U.S. Interest in MFOs and SFOs is still rising.

**The Hanson family office** (pp. 363-364). The U.S. model is seen worldwide as the modern holistic standard. After selling a large operating business in the late 1970s, the Hansons consolidated their wealth into an office that now serves 17 families across four generations, led by a non-family CEO with six staff and a chairperson who oversees direction and succession. No family members work there. Its eight service areas: governance, education, philanthropy, investments, generational wealth planning, tax management, risk management and individual financial planning. It outsources investment managers and prefers passive investing (it once ran in-house strategies). Education aims to make members independent of the family money yet financially empowered, tailored to age, career and family stage, which is delicate because households' means differ. New spouses get an onboarding process with a communication plan. It spends 20-30% of its time on non-financial matters. The CEO says the office must lead the family, not just act out its wishes, and keep proving its value. Next steps: a family values statement and a strategic plan. The lesson: beating generational mathematics is as much about preparing stewards as protecting assets.

## Questions
1. The Hanson office says it must lead the family, not just carry out its wishes, and it spends 20-30% of its time on non-financial issues. Why would a family that sold its business in the 1970s still need that kind of office, and what would go wrong if it became a pure investment shop?
2. In 2012 Li Ka-shing announced his succession plan: elder son Victor would take over most of the business empire, while younger son Richard would get financial backing to build his own ventures. Compare this with the chapter's description of Chinese family decision-making and the commingling of business and family. What makes it a thoughtful plan, and what risks remain?
3. Hong Kong and Singapore have become hubs for family offices serving families from across Asia. Using this lesson, give three reasons an Indonesian or Indian business family might set up its family office in one of those cities rather than at home, and one cultural reason it might resist.

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Generational mathematics: 17 families and four generations need governance, education and spouse onboarding to stay cohesive (pp. 363-364).
- Different means across households require careful information exchange (p. 364).
- A pure investment shop would lose the human-capital work (stewardship, education) that combats dissipation (p. 364).
- The office has to prove its value and lead strategically: a values statement and strategic plan (p. 364).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Chinese families centralize decisions in the founder and are now balancing legacy among siblings (p. 359).
- The plan separates who runs the core business from who is funded to be entrepreneurial, reducing commingled conflict at the ownership level (p. 359).
- It links to Chapter 13: backing a child's own ventures is family-bank-like support.
- Risks: perceived fairness, family harmony, future generations' rights, and loss of face. Governance and communication are still needed.
3. Strong answer covers:
- Hubs offer financial centers with banks, law and accounting firms; multi-jurisdictional planning; stability and accessibility (p. 361).
- Separating business from wealth management as liquid wealth grows (p. 361).
- Access to talent (scarce elsewhere, p. 360) and confidentiality and security (p. 361).
- Resistance: decisions centralized in the family; reluctance to hand family matters to outsiders (Indian values, p. 361); privacy and spreading assets so no one sees the whole (p. 360).

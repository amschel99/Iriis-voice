## The lesson
"The basic question is what vision do you aspire to?" asked Abraham Maslow. Rosplock asks you to imagine your family 10, 20, 50, even 200 years from now. How do members work together? Is there a strong sense of togetherness? What is everyone's health, intellectual and financial well-being? Walk into your future family office: what stayed the same, and what changed? That is visioning, building an inspired picture of where the family and its office want to be.

**Why vision pays.** Mark Lipton, citing Collins and Porras, reports a study comparing visionary firms with similar firms that had no vision. One dollar invested in the general stock market in 1926 grew to $415 by 1990. In the comparison firms it grew to $995. In the visionary firms it grew to $6,356: more than 6 times the comparison group and 15 times the market. A Shareholders Survey Inc. study also found long-term vision was what shareholders cared about most when choosing companies. Family offices are business enterprises too, so why shouldn't this apply to them? Academic research on visionary family offices barely exists. Most vision research covers traditional companies.

**What a vision is.** It sets out the family's preferred future, it reflects the family's values, and it inspires stakeholders (family members, and the office's staff) to do their best. It's like an anchor thrown far into the future that pulls everyone forward. The formula: **Vision = Mission + Strategy + Culture.**

A JP Morgan Private Bank study found eight best practices of families who manage wealth well over the long term. First on the list: articulate a clear and powerful vision. The rest: cultivate entrepreneurial strengths; plan strategically to reduce risk and capture opportunity; build unifying structures that connect family, assets and environment; clarify roles and responsibilities; communicate, communicate, communicate; help members develop competencies; and provide independence, including exit options.

**Three elements that make visioning work:**
1. **Engagement and committing to the clan.** Get everyone to the table and make it inclusive.
2. **Stewardship.** Responsibility for future generations, not just today's needs.
3. **"Hastening slowly."** Methodically weighing each decision moves you faster than rushing.

**Jay Hughes on commitment.** In his interview, Hughes said that when a family's vision is to exist 150 years from now, it *chooses* to be together because it believes it will succeed better together than apart. The family office's vision must hang down from the family's vision, because the office exists to carry it out. The core paradox: through a shared journey in which individuals give up some freedom, they become freer. The benefits include access to investment managers, pooled vehicles, lower costs, more transparency and information, better understanding of extended-family planning, plus the qualitative gains: bonds between siblings and cousins, a support network in crises, a deeper pool of human, social and intellectual capital, and a sense of community.

How does commitment to the clan form? Often implicitly, through rituals (gatherings, working together, social occasions) and rites of passage: births, weddings, anniversaries, degrees, promotions, and also losses such as death, divorce, job loss and illness. Overlapping individual and family experiences build each person's tie to the clan. For founders, the challenge is to model inclusion and joint decision-making, and to get trusted advisors and fiduciaries to see the opportunity in a shared vision.

A key warning: **shared ownership does not mean cohesion.** "Familiness" (or, in Hughes's words, a "family of affinity") takes regular deposits of time, energy and commitment into the family's human capital: meetings, shared experiences, retreats, mentoring, and celebrating accomplishments and history.

Hughes's second point is governance. Families who co-create a vision well can make *joint decisions*. They include everyone, see other points of view, celebrate their differences and work through conflict, rather than letting one person's view carry all the weight.

**Vision versus strategic plan.** An executive the book calls "Brian," who has advised a single family office for nearly 30 years, says the family isn't responsible for the strategic plan. That's the CEO's job. The family is responsible for the *vision*. Vision is *what* the office should help the family do more of, better or differently. Strategy is *how*. Peter Drucker: "If you want to know what the future is, be part of its development."

**The Pitcairn case.** John Pitcairn, a Scottish immigrant, co-founded Pittsburgh Plate Glass (now PPG Industries) in 1883 and became one of the wealthiest men of his time. Above all he held to family, faith, honesty and integrity. A family story: in 1874, during a pricing and contract dispute between independent oil operators led by Pitcairn and Rockefeller's Standard Oil, the lawyers couldn't agree. Rockefeller announced that out of respect for Pitcairn, Standard Oil would be bound by whatever Pitcairn decided was fair. Pitcairn examined the records and set the amount, and Standard Oil paid without argument. In 1986, led by Dirk Jungé (4th generation) and his cousin Feodor Pitcairn (3rd generation, chairman), the family held a low-tech values meeting with members wearing every hat (owner, shareholder, employee, trustee, beneficiary). The mission: "to provide quality financial products and services and achieve superior investment returns." The principles: concern for Pitcairn family values; high standards of ethics and integrity; client oriented; a professionally managed team with entrepreneurial spirit. In 1990 a legacy film brought the values to life. In 2012-2013, celebrating 90 years as a family office and 25 as an MFO, more than 100 of the 120+ family members across three generations used values cards in groups of 8-10, with technology sorting the results in real time. Employees, independent board members, partners and client families did the same. The new commitment statement: building partnerships with multigenerational families; unparalleled advice and service; thought leadership; an independent, prosperous boutique, practising integrity and trust, mastery and excellence, collaborative partnerships, continuous learning and entrepreneurial spirit. Jungé: the 1986 core values were *validated again*.

**Creating your vision: a retreat of 1-3 days**, often planned by a committee or family council, ideally with a facilitator. Start planning 3-6 months ahead and avoid holidays and celebrations.
- **Step 1, education:** explain why each person is invited and what to expect (what is a family office, why me, what if I've never been involved, what will we do with the results).
- **Step 2, preplanning:** send questions 2-3 weeks before the retreat.
- **Step 3, staging:** a family property or a neutral hotel near an airport; get family leadership's buy-in; put a member of each generation on the planning committee; visit the site; set an agenda with breaks; open with an icebreaker.
- **Step 4, develop the vision:** half a day to several days, depending on numbers, what already exists and buy-in.
- **Step 5, align with a strategic plan:** the executives and board turn the vision into a plan over several months. The board asks where the office's operations meet or miss the vision, then reviews and approves the plan and tells the family.

Stephen Covey: "Personal leadership is the process of keeping your vision and values before you and aligning your life to be congruent with them."

## Try it
**Plan a mini vision retreat on paper (pp. 84-87).**
1. Imagine your family in 50 years. Write five sentences: how members work together, health and learning, the enterprise, the family office, what stayed the same.
2. Pick three preplanning questions from Step 2 to send to family members, e.g. "Why do we want to set up or enhance a family office?", "What core services should it provide?", "How will we measure its success and keep it accountable?"
3. Draft a one-page agenda: an icebreaker, a values recap, vision building, then a session on next steps.
4. Split your list into WHAT (the vision, owned by the family) and HOW (the strategic plan, owned by the executives and board).

## Questions
1. In the Pitcairn case, the 2012-2013 process revalidated values first named in 1986, even though the family had sold its core business, become an MFO and changed its investment platform. What does that tell you about the difference between values, mission and vision, and why did the family include employees and independent board members?
2. In 2010-2011 the Hermès family, facing LVMH's surprise stake-building, pooled a majority of their shares into a family holding company that members agreed to keep, rather than each branch keeping full freedom to sell. Use Jay Hughes's idea that "individuals give up some freedom to become freer" to explain this choice. What would a family need already in place for a move like that to hold?
3. Bessemer Trust began as Henry Phipps's family office and opened to other families in 1975. If you were "Brian," the CEO, how would you separate the family's vision from your strategic plan in a change that big? Who decides what, and in which steps of the retreat process?

   - Values are enduring bedrock; the mission and statement changed (from "superior investment returns" to partnerships and service for multigenerational families) as strategy changed (pp. 82-84).
   - Vision = Mission + Strategy + Culture: strategy shifts, values anchor (p. 77).
   - Jungé: anchoring to core values in times of change is powerful (pp. 83-84).
   - Employees and the board were included because the office must hold the same values as the family, or conflict follows (p. 75), and it linked the values to the office's business mission (p. 83).
   - Broad participation (100+ of 120+) builds buy-in (p. 83).
2. Strong answer covers:
   - Hughes: a shared journey where individuals give up freedom makes them freer; joining together beats going one's own way (p. 78).
   - Benefits of the clan: scale, control, protection and a shared future (pp. 78-79).
   - Needed beforehand: a family of affinity with real cohesion (shared ownership alone isn't enough, pp. 79-80); joint decision-making and governance (p. 80); a shared vision and values.
   - Rituals and communication that keep members committed (p. 79).
3. Strong answer covers:
   - The family sets the vision (the what: e.g. serve other families, sustain the office); the CEO and executives build the strategic plan (the how) (p. 80).
   - Retreat steps: educate (1), preplan with questions (2), stage (3), develop the vision (4), then align with the strategic plan, with board review and approval (5) (pp. 84-87).
   - The board asks about gaps between current operations and the vision (p. 87).
   - Hasten slowly; months of iteration (pp. 77, 87).

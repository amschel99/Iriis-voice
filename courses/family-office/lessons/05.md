## The lesson
Chapter 3 opens with Lewis Carroll: "If you don't know where you are going, any road will lead you there." Before picking a solution, you need to know which needs are **must-haves** (acute to the family) and which are **nice-to-haves** (p. 41). This morning covers the landscape and the six archetypes. Tonight covers single vs multi-family offices and how to interview providers.

**The market in numbers (p. 42).**
- **Single family offices (SFOs):** an estimated 2,500–3,000, with over $1.2 trillion.
- **Multi-family offices (MFOs):** about 150, with $400+ billion.

Growing MFOs (Pitcairn, GenSpring, Laird Norton Tyee) publish research. FOX, the Family Wealth Alliance and the Institute for Private Investors educate families. Media such as Campden, Family Office Review, Private Asset Management and Family Wealth Report, and journals such as the *Financial Analyst Journal*, *Trusts & Estates*, *Journal of Wealth Management* and *Family Business Review*, all raise awareness. Academics have started to study family offices too. Much of the visibility comes from MFO marketing.

**Four trends (pp. 43–44):**
1. **Complexity and cost.** Families focus on the cost of running these entities. Complexity comes from the holdings, family relationships and the professional services needed. When there are many trusts, partnerships and businesses, and heirs scattered across countries, a central dedicated office becomes more attractive.
2. **Advisors on *your* side of the table.** Families want advice, not product sales; aligned values; transparency; and no commissions or kickbacks. After Madoff, everyone asks, "Who can I really trust?" Understand how advisors are paid. One investor said that advisors were chanting the same mantra after the 2008 crash as before it, so how could he trust them? The lesson: a trusted advisor still needs to be checked. **71%** of FOX respondents keep discretion over investment decisions. Families also increasingly trust *peer* families for co-investing: **49%** were interested in direct private-equity deals with other families.
3. **Economies of scale.** Investing together opens doors at better rates. Grouping branches raises the average wealth per person, and solidarity matters because firms struggle to define "a family" as a client.
4. **Blurring lines between SFO and MFO.** Overhead rises every year, so many SFOs plan to outsource some or all services to MFO platforms. One executive said: if we can't provide the best, most cost-effective service in-house, we partner with those who can. Often an office has just one or two staff plus a bank, an accountancy firm, estate lawyers, an MFO and consultants.

**The six SFO archetypes (pp. 45–48):**
1. **Multi-generational focused.** Serves a growing, complex group across branches with the full range: lifestyle, investment, tax, estate, wealth transfer, fiduciary, documents, risk, governance, financial education, philanthropy. Members' needs differ, so it may outsource some pieces to an MFO. It is proactive about wealth transfer, with sophisticated governance (a mission or values statement, a constitution, a family board or council, a foundation board). Its strengths are continuity, cohesion and buy-in.
2. **Investment.** Centralizes investing in public and private markets. It provides a coordinated, consistent process, strategic plus tactical allocation, consolidated reporting with performance attribution, tax-aware investing and asset location, liquidity management, and investment partnerships that pool capital. It often holds lots of real estate and private equity. Hedge-fund icons such as **George Soros, Carl Icahn and Stanley Druckenmiller** turned their funds into investment family offices because disclosure rules under **Dodd-Frank** are far lighter for family offices.
3. **Founder's office.** Born from an entrepreneur's success and centered on the founder, the "second coming of the family business." It focuses on architecture, legal structures and managing wealth outside the business. The challenge is when the founder steps back and has to win buy-in from the next owners. Success means empowering non-family leaders and qualified family members.
4. **Administrative and compliance.** Oversight, recordkeeping and a central repository of documents. It is geared to compliance and risk mitigation. It oversees tax returns, banking, insurance and providers, and acts as gatekeeper. Investing is usually *outsourced*. Core services are consolidated, tax and performance reporting, bill pay, lifestyle, tax payments, estate and documents. Concierge services include property, travel, collectibles, gifts, household staff and jets.
5. **Family business-centered.** Starts inside the business. When liquidity builds, it becomes a separate legal entity in a separate location for privacy, security and risk reasons. It handles wealth transfer, tax and diversification away from the company, plus ownership-control strategy, stock repurchase, buy/sell agreements, debt, liquidity, and advice on restructuring or selling. It offers financial planning, cash-flow and capital sufficiency analysis, estate and gift planning, asset protection and governance, all with a multi-generational view.
6. **Legacy and philanthropic.** Prepares heirs and focuses on philanthropy, estate, financial, education and governance planning. The foundation is central and a training ground for leadership. Grant-making can be formal or informal. These families are bonded by purpose and want to honor the story of how the wealth was made. Investing is usually outsourced.

**Determining your needs (p. 48).**
- **Step 1:** review the service types (Chapter 1) and these archetypes, and pick what fits.
- **Step 2:** look at how each service would be performed, how important it is to *all* members versus individual households, and the in-house vs outsourced cost.

Most real offices are a blend, but naming your dominant archetype clarifies what to build first.

## Notes
- Must-haves (acute) vs nice-to-haves; know the destination first (p. 41)
- About 2,500–3,000 SFOs with $1.2T+; about 150 MFOs with $400B+ (p. 42)
- Trend 1: complexity and cost of the structure and services (p. 43)
- Trend 2: advisors on your side of the table; know how they're paid; trust but verify; 71% keep investment discretion; 49% interested in direct private-equity deals with peer families (pp. 43–44)
- Trend 3: economies of scale by pooling branches (p. 44)
- Trend 4: SFOs outsourcing to MFOs; lean staff plus outside providers (p. 44)

- Founder's office challenge: transition and buy-in when the founder steps back (p. 46)
- Investing is usually outsourced in the administrative and legacy archetypes (pp. 47–48)
- Needs analysis: services × importance (all vs some households) × in-house vs outsourced cost (p. 48)

## Try it
**The archetype mix.** Give your family (or one you know) 100 points to split across the six archetypes according to what it actually needs: for example, 40 investment, 30 business-centered, 20 legacy, 10 admin. Then list each service it would need. For each, mark (a) must-have or nice-to-have, (b) for all households or one, and (c) in-house or outsourced. That table is your first draft of a family office design.

## Questions
1. A founder still runs her company and has a growing office that she personally directs. Which archetype is this, what is its biggest risk according to the chapter, and what would you put in place now to manage it?
2. Bernard Madoff's Ponzi scheme collapsed in 2008, and many wealthy families and foundations lost money with him. How does the chapter's second trend (advisors on your side of the table, compensation, trust but verify) change how a family office should pick and monitor managers?
3. The Walton family holds its Walmart stake through a family holding company (Walton Enterprises) and is also known for large-scale philanthropy. Which archetypes would describe its family office needs? Give reasons for at least two.

## Answer key
1.
- The founder's family office: the second coming of the family business, with control centered on the founder (p. 46).
- Biggest risk: when the founder steps back, winning buy-in from the next generation of owners (p. 46).
- Fix: empower qualified non-family leaders and educated family members now.
- Start governance and succession planning (Chapters 11–12).

2.
- Families want advisors who sit on their side of the table, with transparency and no conflicts (commissions, kickbacks) (p. 43).
- Understand how advisors are paid to understand their incentives (p. 43).
- A trusted advisor still needs checking: verify what they do and how (p. 44).
- Ongoing scrutiny and oversight; independent custody and verification (the RFP questions tonight; Chapter 7).
- Book facts: Madoff forced investors to ask "Who can I really trust?"; 71% keep discretion (p. 44).

3.
- Family business-centered: holding company ownership-control strategy and a multi-generational view (p. 47).
- Legacy/philanthropic: foundation-centered, purpose-driven (p. 48).
- Possibly multi-generational focused, given many heirs and branches (p. 45).
- Strong answers justify each choice and note that real offices are blends.
- Keep the facts conservative: a holding company and philanthropy.

## The lesson
Chapter 7 is written by two Withers Worldwide lawyers, David S. Guin and Mark J. Tice. It moves from "how do we organise ourselves?" to "what does the government require of us?" This morning covers the new rules and the big exemption. Tonight covers the reporting duties that apply even when you are exempt.

**What changed: Dodd-Frank (p. 142).** The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act was signed on July 21, 2010. Its Title IV amended the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 and told the SEC to write new exemption rules. It repealed the old **Private Adviser Exemption**, which exempted any adviser with **fewer than 15 clients**. Most family offices had quietly relied on it. In its place came the **Family Office Rule**. A family office that fits the SEC's definition is exempt from registering as an investment adviser. One that doesn't fit must register and be regulated, unless another exemption applies. On the commodities side, the CFTC issued a no-action letter: a family office that meets the SEC's rule won't be regulated as a **commodity pool operator**.

**What did NOT change (pp. 142-143).** Three older rules still apply:
- **Beneficial ownership reporting.** Big holders, directors and officers of public companies must disclose. This can expose individual family members' holdings, which matters to families who value privacy.
- **Institutional investment manager reports.** Anyone exercising discretion over **$100 million** or more of "Section 13(f) securities" reports periodically to the SEC.
- **Large trader reports.** These cover accounts with very big daily or monthly trading.

The SEC says these reports exist to monitor systemic risk. Many 13F reports can be treated confidentially, and large trader filings are non-public. Insider trading enforcement also continues. Family members who pick up confidential information at home are targets, and so is the relative who left it lying around.

**The Smith Family Office structure (pp. 143-145).** The authors use one example structure all chapter, so learn it now. From the bottom up:
- **InvestCo A** and **InvestCo B** are Delaware LLCs, the family investment vehicles.
- Their sole member is **IntermediaryCo**, a Delaware corporation.
- IntermediaryCo is wholly owned by **Family OfficeCo**, a Delaware corporation whose directors are Jennifer Smith (the matriarch) and her husband Joe.
- Family OfficeCo is wholly owned by **TrustCo**, a South Dakota private trust company.
- TrustCo is wholly owned by **The Smith Trust**, a revocable trust. Jennifer is the grantor and Joe is the trustee.

**The single family office exemption (pp. 145-148).** To qualify, three things must be true:

1. **Owned by family clients and exclusively controlled by family members and/or family entities.**
   - **Family members** are the lineal descendants of a common ancestor, including adopted, step and foster children, plus their spouses or spousal equivalents. The common ancestor can be no more than **10 generations** above the youngest generation.
   - **Family entities** are the trusts, estates, companies and similar entities that count as family clients. Key employees and their trusts don't count when you decide whether an entity is a family entity.
   - **"Exclusively controlled"** in practice means the family is *in fact* in control. Majority family control of management or policy is enough, according to the SEC staff FAQ.
   - Two examples show the line. A board with a family majority passes, as long as no side arrangement hands control to outsiders. A board made up entirely of non-family members fails, *even if* the family appoints and can fire them, unless another arrangement leaves management and policy decisions to the family shareholders.
   - Because professional staff run many offices day to day and outsiders often sit on boards, a lawyer should review the organisational documents.

2. **Advice only to family clients.** The list is broad:
   - family members and former family members;
   - key employees and certain former key employees;
   - charities funded *exclusively* by family clients;
   - certain, but not all, trusts tied to family clients (ask counsel);
   - trusts where only key employees are trustees or decision makers, and each contributor is a key employee or, in some cases, that employee's spouse;
   - the estates of these people;
   - companies wholly owned by, and run only for, family clients.

3. **No holding out to the public as an investment adviser.** The SEC reads this broadly. Even putting "investment adviser" on business cards or stationery, or describing US advisory work on a website, can count.

**Where offices trip up (p. 147).** The common failures are:
- advising a pooled vehicle partly owned by outsiders;
- advising family friends or key employees' relatives (who are not family clients);
- acting as a multi-family office.

**Applying it to the Smiths (pp. 148-149).** Family OfficeCo advises InvestCo A and B. Follow the ownership up: TrustCo is wholly owned by The Smith Trust, a revocable trust whose sole grantor is a family member, so TrustCo is a family client. InvestCo A and B sit under IntermediaryCo, so they are ultimately owned by the trust too. Everything qualifies.

Now change one fact. If **Jennifer's brother-in-law** owned part of InvestCo A, the exemption would fail, because **in-laws are not family members**. Or suppose a foundation Family OfficeCo advises takes outside money. It stops being a family client, and the exemption fails again. Note 8 adds that charities with old outside money had until December 31, 2013 to spend it.

The lesson for you: one small outsider anywhere in the structure can force SEC registration.

## Notes
- Dodd-Frank was signed July 21, 2010; Title IV amended the Investment Advisers Act of 1940 (p. 142).
- The repealed Private Adviser Exemption covered advisers with fewer than 15 clients; the Family Office Rule replaced it for family offices (p. 142).
- CFTC no-action letter: an SEC-exempt family office is not a commodity pool operator (p. 142).
- Unchanged duties: beneficial ownership reporting, 13F (institutional investment managers with $100M+ in 13(f) securities), large trader reports, insider trading enforcement (pp. 142-143).
- Smith structure: Smith Trust (revocable; Jennifer grantor, Joe trustee) → TrustCo (SD private trust company) → Family OfficeCo (DE; directors Jennifer and Joe) → IntermediaryCo (DE) → InvestCo A and B (DE LLCs) (pp. 143-145).
- Exemption test: owned by family clients, exclusively controlled by family members or entities, advice only to family clients, no holding out (pp. 145-148).
- Family members: lineal descendants of a common ancestor no more than 10 generations above the youngest, plus spouses and spousal equivalents, including former spouses (p. 145).
- "Exclusive control" means majority family control in fact; a board of family-appointed outsiders alone fails (pp. 145-147).
- Family clients include key employees, family-funded charities, certain trusts, estates and wholly owned companies (p. 147).
- Holding out includes business cards, stationery and websites (pp. 147-148).
- Typical failures: outside investors in a vehicle, advising friends or key employees' relatives, multi-family service (p. 147).
- In-laws are not family members; an outside-funded foundation is not a family client (p. 149).

## Try it
Run the **Family Office Rule check** (the flow chart on p. 146) on your own family, or on a family you might advise:
1. Name the common ancestor. Is the youngest generation within 10 generations?
2. List every entity the office advises: trusts, LLCs, foundations, holding companies. For each, note who owns it and who funded it. Circle any non-family owner, in-law, friend or employee's relative.
3. Who controls the office's management and policies? Draw the board. Is the family majority real, or only the power to appoint outsiders?
4. Check the website, business cards and letterhead for anything that "holds out" the office as an adviser.
5. Decide: exempt, needs restructuring, or must register.

## Questions
1. In the Smith structure, Jennifer wants to invite her brother-in-law to put $5 million into InvestCo A, and she wants the family foundation to accept a gift from a close friend. Walk through what each move does to the family office exemption, and suggest how she could get the same result without losing it.
2. Real world: in 2011, as Dodd-Frank's registration rules approached, George Soros's firm and Carl Icahn's firm both returned outside investors' money and became family offices. Using this morning's lesson, explain why returning the money was the step that made them exempt, and what they gave up in exchange.
3. Real world: Archegos Capital Management was Bill Hwang's family office. It collapsed in March 2021 and caused billions of dollars in losses at banks. As a family office it was not a registered investment adviser. Argue both sides: should the family office exemption exist for an office managing billions with heavy borrowing? What would you add to the rule, if anything, without hurting ordinary families?

## Answer key
1. Brother-in-law and friend:
   - In-laws aren't "family members" (only lineal descendants and their spouses), so his stake makes InvestCo A a non-family client, and advising it breaks the exemption (pp. 145, 149).
   - A foundation funded by an outsider is no longer a family client, so it breaks too (pp. 147, 149).
   - Ways out: the brother-in-law's *spouse*, Jennifer's sibling, is a lineal descendant of a common ancestor and may invest, depending on who the defined common ancestor is. Alternatively keep his money in a separate vehicle the office does not advise. The friend could give to a public charity or donor-advised fund instead.
   - Always have counsel review the structure (p. 147).
2. Soros and Icahn:
   - The Private Adviser Exemption for advisers with fewer than 15 clients was repealed, so large hedge funds would have had to register (p. 142).
   - The Family Office Rule exempts offices that advise only family clients and are family controlled; outside investors aren't family clients (pp. 145-147).
   - Returning the money removed every non-family client, so they qualified.
   - Trade-off: they lost fee income and outside capital, and they still face 13D/13G, 13F, large trader reporting and insider trading law (pp. 142-143). The book notes hedge-fund icons converting on p. 46.
3. Archegos:
   - Exemption rationale: the SEC doesn't want to regulate private families' investing, and families value privacy (pp. 166-167).
   - Counter-argument: an office that size with heavy leverage creates systemic risk for the banks it trades with. The reporting rules exist to monitor systemic risk (p. 143).
   - The book's warning applies: regulators won't allow loopholes that professionals can use (p. 167).
   - Good answers propose proportionate fixes, such as size or leverage thresholds, or derivatives and swap exposure counted in disclosures, while keeping privacy for ordinary family offices.
   - Note that the rules may have changed since the book (2014).

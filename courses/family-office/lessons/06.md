## The lesson
Tonight's question: build your own single family office (SFO), or join a multi-family office (MFO)? Then, how do you grill the providers?

**Why an SFO? Strengths (pp. 48–50).**
- **Control and privacy, the two biggest drivers.** Most SFO wealth came from a business sale, a partial liquidity event, a big executive package or a windfall. The first-generation creator is used to leading, sees the wealth as a *new business*, and likes being able to customize. Families choose obscure names and stay under the radar, with the office as gatekeeper.
- **No competition between SFOs.** Because they don't compete, they share best practices. One offered to share an expensive in-house reporting system. A Chicago peer group of SFOs agreed that if a key executive became incapacitated, the others would step in short-term and help search for a successor.
- **Total alignment** with family values. Conflicts like product commissions are rare. The dedicated, integrated service "can be worth the premium."
- **Patient capital.** A decades-long horizon and a "stay rich, not get rich" mindset that favors preservation over get-rich-quick schemes.

Full list (Figure 3.1): control, customized dedicated service, privacy and anonymity, alignment and integration, no SFO competition, patient capital, wealth preservation, family first.

**SFO weaknesses (pp. 50–52).**
- **Talent:** few people are trained for family office work, and there's no career ladder (Chapter 10).
- **Rising costs:** these grow while the wealth may shrink generation by generation.
- **Operating costs:** generally **150–200 basis points (1.5–2%)** of assets, and somewhat higher for SFOs of $100M or less. The cost-to-scale trade-off can deter cost-sensitive families. Others happily pay for control, privacy, customization and objective advice.
- **The Family Office Expense Ratio (FOER).** One office tracks it monthly as partnership expenses (including manager fees) divided by average net assets, annualized. For January to June: year-to-date expenses ÷ (net assets at month-end ÷ 6) gives the average balance; annualize by dividing by 6 and multiplying by 12. Variations exist. Don't confuse it with a mutual fund's operating expense ratio (OER).
- **Direct and variable costs.** Direct costs are talent, operations, infrastructure, technology, reporting, administration and risk. Variable costs are asset fees, custody and outside professionals.

Full list of weaknesses: talent, cost control, access to products and services, operational efficiency, service gaps, bridging family continuity, urgency in strategic planning.

**SFO opportunities and threats (pp. 52–53).**
- **Opportunities:** a growing "underground network" of SFOs, including informal peer groups and emergency plans; co-investing (Chapter 8); sharing research and manager due diligence; sharing a consultant's cost across offices; open architecture; first-class service.
- **Threats:** expenses, family expectations and **scope creep**. Scope creep means services that cost more in-house than outsourced, get added bit by bit without being monitored, or are unbalanced between households. Other threats: keeping the family engaged, family conflict, compensation, generational mathematics and succession.

**Why an MFO? (pp. 53–56).** An MFO serves anywhere from 2–3 to more than 500 families. FOX finds an average of 83 client relationships, with median investable assets of $20M and median net worth of $50M.
- **Fees:** a fee on assets, project or hourly fees, or a retainer plus an asset fee. Fees rise with accounts, legal complexity, households, reporting, custodians, advisors, transactions, countries and beneficiaries.
- **Core services:** cash flow, estate, tax, financial planning, investing, education, philanthropy, family meeting facilitation. Others: fiduciary, insurance, credit, tax preparation, banking, trust administration, bill pay, concierge, art and property.
- **Growth drivers:** buy instead of build; delegate and centralize; cost savings.
- **Strengths:** scaled expertise. A single family office rarely hires a family-dynamics or governance expert full time; a large MFO often has one. MFOs also have deep investment teams, better pay packages (salary, bonus, long-term equity), client teams of 2–5+ (experts plus relationship managers plus junior staff), investment in infrastructure (reporting, secure documents, family websites) and experience across many families.
- **Weaknesses:** profit motive (watch the client-to-advisor ratio), less high-touch service, more limited products if institutionalized, middleman fees on outsourced pieces, and marketing costs. "I have yet to meet an SFO with a marketing department." Others: group-think, less customization.
- **Opportunities:** more for less, a learning ground before building your own SFO, a one-stop shop, shared specialist costs, access to managers and research, innovation.
- **Threats:** growing too fast through acquisitions or mergers, which strains teams and dilutes service; overpromising; staff turnover; ownership by banks or institutions pushing products; corporate culture clashes (Figure 3.2).

**Questions to ask any provider (pp. 56–61):**
- **Scope:** exactly what will be delivered and how? Ask for a sample engagement letter. Which services are outsourced? Interview the third parties. How is the firm better than its peers?
- **Who you'll work with:** the C-suite pitches, but find out who actually serves you. Ask for résumés, run background checks, ask how many clients each manager handles and what counts as capacity. "No limit" is a red flag.
- **History:** read Form ADV at adviserinfo.sec.gov. Who owns the firm and its affiliates? What stage is it at (under 5 years, 5–15, over 15)? How has it grown, organically or by acquisition? What is the 5-, 10- and 25-year plan? What share of the budget goes to PR and marketing? Any lawsuits or arbitration? Ask for a copy of its liability insurance.
- **Clients:** typical profile, boutique vs mid-size vs large, assets and clients won and lost, published rankings, how satisfaction is measured.
- **Your top 3 must-haves:** how have they solved them? Ask for a failure story; a fake-perfect answer is a red flag. Ask for references who've been clients for more than 3–5 years.
- **Privacy:** how often are measures audited? How many staff can see your data? Have they signed NDAs? What happens to your data when you leave? Check the news for breaches.
- **Security:** physical documents, location risks (floods, hurricanes, blackouts), cybersecurity testing, an annual disaster-recovery drill with test scripts.
- **Conflicts:** compensation is the number one source. Multiple revenue streams or referral fees mean you should probe further.
- **Communication:** reporting vs face time, meeting frequency, sample reports, digital access for the younger generation.
- **Regulators:** SEC and state registration; the FDIC, Federal Reserve or OCC for banks. Verify balances directly with custodians, and consider an unannounced audit.
- **Fiduciary vs Suitability standard.** Fiduciary means client first, care and prudence, full disclosure, avoiding or disclosing conflicts, and the burden on the advisor to prove they acted in your interest; duties to disclose fees, control expenses, document due diligence and diversify. Suitability means "arm's length": products can favor the firm, and the burden is on the investor.

**RFP (pp. 62–65).** Families, often helped by a consultant acting as their agent, send written questions. Firms reply in 2–4+ weeks, then come face-to-face meetings. Courting can take weeks or months, with fits and starts, because switching is costly: exit fees, new custody, liquidations, weeks to months to move. Chemistry matters as much as technical skill. FOX's RFP template covers:
- **firm background:** founding, structure, ownership, strategic plan, mission, RIA status, Form ADV;
- **services:** wealth transfer, financial planning, philanthropy, investing, performance, tax, bill pay, education, strengths, what's new, outside consultants;
- **client relationships:** counts, types, wins and losses and why, business development and how it's paid, assets under management and its growth, 3 references with 3+ years;
- **employees:** headcount by role, team structure, manager pay and incentives, caseload, bios, hires and departures over 3 years;
- **servicing and reporting:** online access, sample redacted reports;
- **education and research;**
- **pricing:** fee schedule, minimums, performance fees, soft dollars, other payments. "If my sister and I join, are we one family for pricing?"

## Try it
1. **Compute a FOER.** Suppose an office has year-to-date (Jan–Jun) expenses including manager fees of $1.2M, and month-end net assets of $150M. Average balance = $150M ÷ 6 = $25M. Ratio = $1.2M ÷ $25M = 4.8%. Annualized ÷ 6 × 12 = 9.6%. That shows how the book's monthly method behaves; try it with your own numbers and compare with the 1.5–2% benchmark. (Note: the book's formula is ambiguous. A simpler check is annualized expenses ÷ average assets: $2.4M ÷ $150M = 1.6%.)
2. **Draft an RFP.** Write your top three must-haves, then pick 10 questions from FOX's template you'd send first. Include the fiduciary-vs-suitability question and "what happens to my data when I leave?"

## Questions
1. An SFO managing $90 million spends about $2.5 million a year and keeps adding concierge services for one branch. Using the cost benchmark, the FOER idea and the definition of scope creep, what's wrong, and what options from the chapter should the family consider?
2. Bessemer Trust grew from the Phipps family's office into a firm serving thousands of families. Using the MFO SWOT, what strengths would a family gain by joining a large MFO like this, and which weaknesses and threats should its RFP questions probe?
3. Many US wealth firms have been owned by banks or have been bought and merged into bigger groups. Which MFO threats does this raise, and which specific questions from pp. 56–61 would expose them?

## Answer key
1.
- $2.5M ÷ $90M ≈ 2.8%, above the 1.5–2% benchmark; smaller SFOs (≤$100M) cost more (p. 52).
- Track a FOER (p. 51).
- Scope creep: services added incrementally, unmonitored, unbalanced across households (p. 53).
- Options: outsource to an MFO or providers (SFO/MFO blurring, p. 44); charge households for their usage; cut nice-to-haves; consider joining an MFO.
- Note the threats of keeping family on board and family conflict.

2.
- Strengths: scale, specialists, deep investment teams, infrastructure and reporting, experience across many families (pp. 54–55).
- Weaknesses and threats: client-to-advisor load, less high touch, institutionalized offering, middleman fees, growth strain, overpromising (pp. 55–56).
- RFP questions: caseload limits, team bios and turnover, fee schedule, outsourced services, clients won and lost and why (pp. 57–65).
- Book fact: Bessemer has 2,000+ clients and $57.5B (p. 13).

3.
- Threats: institutional owners pushing products (conflict), culture clashes, growing too fast through acquisitions, talent turnover (pp. 55–56).
- Questions: ownership and affiliates (Form ADV), how the firm grew (organic vs acquisition), revenue streams and referral payments, fiduciary vs suitability, pending lawsuits, staff hires and terminations (pp. 57–61, 63–64).
- Strong answers tie this to "advisors on your side of the table" (p. 43).

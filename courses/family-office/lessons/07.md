## The lesson
Chapter 4 opens with one of the book's strongest claims: understanding a family's values, clarifying its mission and articulating a clear vision are perhaps the biggest differences between families whose family offices outlive one generation of owners and those whose don't. Interview after interview, family office executives said the same thing: the offices that last get a clear direction, purpose and destination from the family. This lesson covers values and mission. Tonight's covers vision.

**What values are.** Values are the governing principles, beliefs and behavioural guidelines that shape how you act and see things. Brian Hall defines them as "ideals that give significance to our lives that are reflected through the priorities that we choose, and that we act on consistently and repeatedly." His point is that values form a hierarchy, ranked by how we prioritise them. They connect to our beliefs, our experiences and even the keepsakes we treasure. In short, values drive your choices, your behaviour and your decisions.

**Valuables versus values.** Franco Lombardo, a Canadian family business coach, notices that families get stuck by focusing more on their *valuables* than their *values*. Objects and possessions feel empty to many of his clients. They don't fill the void that understanding their values can. Net worth can't replace self-worth. Your financial position moves up and down the ladder. Your values are the constant.

**The "soft stuff" is the hard stuff.** Many families avoid values work, calling it too soft. Mark Haynes Daniell quotes an observer: these people issues "aren't 'soft' issues at all. Maybe we should call them the 'even harder' issues." Rosplock compares a first values conversation to stepping off a high diving board: you can't dip a toe in to test the water, so the first jump feels strange. Ellen Miley Perry (*A Wealth of Possibilities*): "To earn trust, one must be vulnerable. It is the only path to real intimacy." Most of us live unaware of our own values, and naming them makes us feel exposed. The good news is that sharing them openly with family breaks old patterns and feedback loops that have blocked cohesion, and opens new lines of communication.

**How families explore values.** The book describes three approaches:
- **Dennis Jaffe's values cards.** Each family member picks values from a deck and tells the stories behind them. Where individual values overlap, you find the family's values, the core of its identity and culture. Jaffe's view: to stay connected across generations, a family needs a shared purpose. Working on values blends the founding generation's legacy with the newer generations' concerns, as a dynamic, cross-generational conversation.
- **Gunther Weil's ValueMentors.** An assessment built on 25 years of research and more than 20,000 people surveyed worldwide, followed by coaching that connects your values to your experiences and actions. Weil calls values a "cultural currency" that everyone understands and exchanges. Rosplock went through it herself and calls it "illuminating and powerful."
- **GenSpring's process.** One-to-one mentoring and values conversations with each member, ending in a family meeting where shared values are revealed. Daisy Medici's key insight is that many family differences come from *different value priorities*, not the malicious intent people often assume. Picture 20 or 30 relatives of different ages, places and backgrounds discovering what they share. It "gets at the soul of the family."

You don't need a big family for this. A single household (parents plus adult children) benefits too. Shared values make the "hot spots" less divisive. Most families prefer an outside facilitator, but do-it-yourselfers can start with the book's starter exercise (see Try it).

**The values statement.** The output is a short statement of the values the family shares and considers core to its identity and culture. Include only those. The sample James Family Values Statement lists:
- **Integrity and honesty**
- **Humbleness and kindness**
- **Strength and perseverance**
- **Family belonging and unity**
- **Social responsibility and stewardship**

**The mission statement.** A mission answers: *Why are we together, and what are we going to achieve as a family?* It states the purpose of keeping the family connected to its wealth and enterprise, and it usually has the values embedded in it. The sample Genson Family mission is "to pursue individual and family happiness through love and meaningful work". It commits the family to: living its shared values (respect, generosity, equality, education, empathy, integrity, accountability), associating with good people, places and things, preserving and passing on intellectual wealth, history and legacy, nurturing and safeguarding all members, working together to create and preserve financial wealth, and enjoying life through good health, behaviour and attitudes. Missions are like signatures: each is unique, but they share common features.

**Why the family office must be in the loop.** Many families outsource values and mission work to a consultant or a multi-family office. When they do, the office's leaders and advisors must still be involved, because these documents guide how the office operates and advises. Some families even assess the values of family office staff. Weil notes that, like DISC and Myers-Briggs (MBTI), a values assessment can be used before hiring to confirm a values match for key executives. The work takes weeks to months. Keep the documents front and centre afterwards, and create rituals that celebrate them. There are three reasons the office must know them: they are its grounding directives, they help advisors teach younger members the purpose of the wealth, and the office exists *because* of the family's commitment to one another. If the office holds different values or a conflicting mission, conflict is likely.

## Try it
**The book's values starter exercise (p. 72-73).** Do it yourself tonight, then with one or two family members this week:
1. Write down the three to five values that matter most to you, and why.
2. For each, recall a specific experience or memory where that value was shown or tested.
3. Write the biggest lesson you took from it and why it still matters today.
4. If others take part, compare lists. Circle the values you share. Those are the raw material for a family values statement like the James family's.
5. Bonus: draft one sentence answering "Why are we together, and what will we achieve?" That's the first line of a mission statement.

## Questions
1. A family hires an MFO to run a values and mission retreat but leaves its own single family office executives out of the process. Using the chapter, explain the risks and what the family should do instead.
2. John D. Rockefeller Sr. was known for frugality, keeping meticulous personal ledgers from his first job, and giving to charity from his youth. Later generations built shared structures such as the Rockefeller Brothers Fund (founded by his grandsons in 1940). How might founder values like these be turned into a written values statement and mission that a family office can act on? Name two values and how each would show up in the office's work.
3. Many famous inheritances end in public lawsuits between siblings. Using Daisy Medici's insight about value priorities, explain how a values process could change the way siblings read each other's motives before a dispute hardens. Which of the three methods in this chapter would you pick for a large, scattered family, and why?

## The lesson
Chapter 14 opens with Stephen Covey: "The mission of life: live, love, learn and leave a legacy." The dictionary calls legacy a bequest of property, or anything handed down from the past (p. 334). Families mean much more by it: an **ethical will** of stories and values, a **financial legacy** of valuables and wealth, or a **philanthropic footprint**. The chapter frames three kinds: financial, social and philanthropic. The family office can play a central role in supporting all three.

**Legacy for the senior generation** (pp. 334-336). Capturing your legacy is a powerful exercise. Through an oral, written or film account, it shows what you endured and the stories that shaped you.
- **Iris Wagner** (Memoirs Productions) says legacy is as much about the *process* of reflecting on what gave life meaning as about the end product. Stories surface that even the children never heard. "Legacy is about linking the past to the present and providing guideposts for future generations." Over generations, captured stories weave a fabric of values.
- **Fredda Herz Brown and Fran Lotery** (*Family Wealth Sustainability Toolkit*): a legacy is "at once timeless and time bound." It is different from what the family owns, and each generation must reshape the vision and values and recommit to the legacy. Legacy is part of long-term sustainability, not just reflection.
- **John A. Warnick** (Purposeful Planning Institute): the purpose and love behind wealth often get buried in the "sterility of the typical legalese of wills and trusts." Don't let the "tax tail wag the rest of the legacy." He has advisors use visioning exercises and purposeful conversations so that love, meaning and wisdom are woven into the estate plan. Heirs who read their parents' heartfelt purposes report "tears of joy," which is a strong antidote to entitlement. Family meetings and storytelling also help.
- **Rick Harig** (Legacy Resources) describes a couple with large, well-structured wealth who found no meaning in it. He moves clients from *transactional* to *transformational* planning: discovering what impact their wealth can have on family and the world.

**Legacy for the next generation** (pp. 336-337). Dr. Carolyn Friend inherited her parents' wealth, but those were just objects; her legacy questions to them would never be answered. With Dr. James Weiner she built the **Legacy Conversation**, which engages both senior members *and their children* in intergenerational dialogue for clarity, harmony and peace of mind. Legacy needn't be one event. Kirby's grandfather self-published a 50- to 60-page book of lessons, quotes and sayings at each milestone birthday (30, 40, 50, 60...) for family and friends. Several families interviewed for the book have the young interview the elders, record or transcribe it, or present what they learned at a family retreat, which is inspiring for the next generation too.

**Gender and legacy** (pp. 337-338). GenSpring's study of 218 people with $1 million+ net worth or large expected inheritances found men and women broadly aligned. Both value financially independent children who earn their own money. Men rate mentoring heirs and financial education somewhat higher. Both value leaving a legacy and deciding what happens to their wealth. But at death, women intend to give about 20% to philanthropy versus 4% for men. Men would leave 20% to siblings and 56% to children and stepchildren, versus 8% and 47% for women.

A Wilmington Trust/Campden qualitative study of 40 women aged 40-65 with $25 million+ found: two children each on average; the top value to pass on is generosity to the community; 95% are in philanthropic families, often with foundations they want their children in; the earlier the wealth came, the more community-minded they were. Other values: respecting people of all financial statuses, a strong work ethic, self-esteem independent of wealth, financial literacy, appreciating wealth without being spoiled, and prudent spending. 90% describe their own spending as below their means (and say their husbands do the same). They worry that openly discussing wealth will undercut prudence, and some fear trusts set up early will stop children becoming independent. 63% intend to leave their children a great deal; 30% a safety net. 88% have high-to-moderate participation in managing family wealth, usually with one especially trusted advisor. They want holistic advisors who educate them and their children and treat them as equal to the men in financial decisions.

**Defining legacy and financial legacy** (pp. 339-340). From Kirby's dissertation: a *financial legacy* is the wealth passed on; a *social legacy* is the values and attitudes modeled; a *philanthropic legacy* is what's left to a cause or charity. On financial legacy:
- 8 in 10 women feel strongly about leaving a legacy and 95% plan to split wealth equally among children; for men it's 78% and 97%. That suggests primogeniture (all to the first-born son) is no longer practiced in the U.S.
- Both envision passing about half their wealth to children and stepchildren; 67% name philanthropic causes as primary beneficiaries. Others: grandchildren, siblings, parents, spouse, church, synagogue.
- 62% of the women intend their children to receive $1-10 million, far less than their wealth: a head start, not an extravagant lifestyle ("let them earn it").
- In the online focus group, all but one valued sustaining wealth across generations. That one would help with a business or a house but didn't feel obliged to fund her lifestyle for them. Four of six wanted to decide where their assets go, mainly to **prevent family disputes**.
- 71% are proud of their family's wealth, but 81% of men versus 46% of women are *more* proud of their financial contributions. 62% prefer others not know they're wealthy; only 16% feel people try to take advantage of them.

## Try it
Run a mini Legacy Conversation (Friend and Weiner's idea, p. 336):
1. Write five questions you'd want a parent or grandparent to answer before it's too late (e.g. "What hardship shaped you most?" "What do you hope our money does?").
2. Ask one elder at least two of them this week and record or note the answers.
3. Draft the first paragraph of your own ethical will: one story, one value it taught you, one hope for those after you.
4. Ask yourself Warnick's test: does your will or trust say *why*, or only *who gets what*?

## Questions
1. Warnick warns against the "tax tail wagging the legacy." Using the GenSpring and Wilmington findings, explain how a family could have a perfect, tax-efficient estate plan and still fail at legacy. What would you add to the documents?
2. In 1889 Andrew Carnegie wrote "The Gospel of Wealth," arguing the rich should give their fortunes away in their lifetimes, and he funded thousands of public libraries. Which of the three legacy types does this represent, and how does his view compare with the study participants who plan to leave about half to their children?
3. In 2010 Bill Gates and Warren Buffett launched the Giving Pledge, asking billionaires to commit the majority of their wealth to philanthropy. Using this lesson's data on how much people plan to leave children versus charity, and the "head start, not a lifestyle" idea, argue whether a pledge like this strengthens or weakens a family's financial and social legacy.

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Documents convey "what you have," not "what you want them to know"; love and purpose get lost in legalese (p. 335).
- Heirs may feel entitlement, or confusion about purpose; Warnick finds purposeful statements reduce entitlement (p. 335).
- The women fear that early trusts undercut independence, and value prudence and work ethic (p. 338).
- Add: an ethical will or purpose statement, family meetings and storytelling, a Legacy Conversation, a values statement (pp. 335-337).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Mainly a philanthropic legacy, with a strong social legacy too (values of public good) (p. 339).
- Contrast: participants envision about half to children and 67% name philanthropy as a beneficiary (p. 339); women plan 20% versus men 4% to philanthropy at death (p. 337).
- Carnegie's dictum: get educated, make money, give it away (p. 384).
- Nuance: the study participants also give a "head start" sum rather than a lifestyle; both reject unearned luxury.
3. Strong answer covers:
- Consistent with the "head start, not a lifestyle" findings (62% of women planning $1-10M per child, p. 339) and the values of independence and earning (pp. 337-338).
- Strengthens social and philanthropic legacy: generosity is the top value women want to pass on (p. 338).
- Risks: heirs' expectations; need for communication to avoid disputes, which motivates four of six to direct their assets (p. 340).
- Balanced judgment, with family involvement in the giving to make it a shared legacy.

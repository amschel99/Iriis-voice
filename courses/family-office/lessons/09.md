## The lesson
Chapter 5 is written by Withers Worldwide lawyers Ivan Sacks, William Kambas and Justin Zamparelli. Their premise: family offices are as unique as the families they serve, so setting one up is a *bespoke* process. Families still ask the same seven questions, though, and working through them shapes the right structure organically:
1. What legal form should the office take?
2. Who pays to establish and run it?
3. Who owns it, and how?
4. Who manages it, and how?
5. How is it structured for tax efficiency?
6. Can it be structured to manage risk?
7. What is the process of establishing it?

Ultimately, family governance that includes the office becomes a way of life, and senior members in particular must understand the whole architecture and each part's role. Today covers questions 1-3. Tonight covers 4-7.

**The case: "the Family".** The grandparents built a market-leading business and put their equity into a complex trust. Their daughter, Mom, ran it and took it to market leadership. Mom and Dad have four college-educated children starting their own families. Big dividends funded an investment programme now nearly as valuable as the business, also held in the trust. They want help with business management and investments, long-term philanthropy (probably a private foundation), and a way to preserve and share the family's history with the grandchildren. They also note that a simple, efficient office might later take in cousins or similar families, growing it into a business that could even be sold to a bank or MFO.

**Question 1: legal form.** Start with the scope of the whole family enterprise, then fit the office's services into it. The Family wants to share costs centrally but allocate them by each person's use. Four initial points:
- **In-house versus outsourcing.** What expertise do you have? What's cheaper outside? Fixed versus variable costs: bill paying could be a flat rate or priced on monthly volume; tax compliance could be priced per entity or per group of entities for each branch.
- **Structuring investments** for venture capital, private equity, non-US investments, real estate, financial instruments, and public stocks and bonds.
- **Relationship with other governance structures** such as trusts and private trust companies (PTCs). Separate or integrated? Owners, governors or clients of the office? A PTC might already do fiduciary work and monitoring, while the office finds investment opportunities, perhaps through a shared investment committee.
- **Taxation** of income and deductions, and whether income or losses stay in the office or flow to the owners.

**Choice of entity.** The options are a partnership, LLC, S corporation or C corporation. Partnerships carry liability problems and S corps restrict flexibility with income and expenses, so most offices today are **LLCs or C corporations**.
- **LLC:** far fewer state formalities. It's managed by members or managers (usually no directors or officers), governed by an Operating Agreement (OA), and formed by filing Articles of Formation (or Organization) with a flat fee. By default it's taxed as a pass-through partnership: no annual federal tax, but federal and state information returns, and possibly state taxes or fees. The OA is drafted at formation and often amended.
- **C corporation:** a separate taxpayer. It's formed by filing Articles of Incorporation with the Secretary of State and governed by state law, the articles and bylaws. Requirements include electing directors and officers, an organisational meeting, bylaws, annual meetings, documented decisions, minutes, annual tax returns and state reports.
- **International wrinkle:** with a UK-resident manager, an LLC would likely be treated as a UK corporation, so a limited partnership may be better.

**The governing instrument** (bylaws or OA) is your chance to protect the family from internal and external threats. It sets the office's services; leadership, management and compensation; how ownership interests are allocated; voting power; owners' and managers' rights and duties; profits and losses by class; and rules for meetings and minutes. It should also protect intellectual property, set non-compete terms, resolve conflicts to avoid litigation, protect directors and managers from liability, protect the business if partners fail, and plan ownership transitions (shareholder rights, buy-sell provisions). It should also reflect family values and the constitution or mission, and record any input from committees or councils.

**Three structural models:**
- **Simple office** (investment and portfolio management): one entity that consolidates performance and reporting and exchanges ideas between the family and its bankers (Fig. 5.1).
- **Angel or venture capitalist office:** the family backs start-ups and private equity, takes board seats, or runs its own fund-of-funds, with a GP/fund management services entity (Fig. 5.2).
- **Complex corporate-conglomerate office:** a holding company headquarters with subsidiaries, e.g. an **IP Co** (licenses family IP), a **Finance Co** (a family bank: borrowing, lending, capital calls, credit lines), and a **Tech Co** (technology and data services), with a non-stock corporation or voting trust on top (Fig. 5.3). This helps with global tax rates, privacy, liability and staffing.

Multiple entities (including brother-sister arrangements) can also insulate one service area from another's creditors, or decentralise across countries.

**Don't over-build.** Complexity adds cost, drags on productivity, is hard to pass on, and can cause estate-tax inclusion in each generation. Start with entities that can be restructured easily and tax-free. The worst case is members who don't follow what's been written down. The rule: **"as simple as possible, but no more simple."** The structure should formalise a decision hierarchy that matches how the family actually decides, have the right number of entities (a real estate family may sensibly have hundreds), minimise income tax, and serve estate goals. (GSTT: the US generation-skipping transfer tax on transfers to people two or more generations below the giver.)

Back to the Family: Mom's custom operations and sales technology may need IP planning, depending on whether it was built for the business or independently. A child active in venture capital or private equity could join a family fund-of-funds GP or act as liaison. A child keen on philanthropy could be the liaison to the foundation.

**Question 2: who pays?** Like any service business, the users pay: overhead is built into fees paid by individuals or family entities, often from investment profits. Start-up capital can be equity (which makes the contributor an owner), debt (repaid, no stake) or a mix. In an LLC taxed as a partnership, expenses flow through to the owners, who may deduct them. A C corp keeps expenses and net operating losses on its own return. Above all, every generation must see costs as fair. A senior generation may choose to pay as a kind of gift. There are four fee models:
1. **Pure billable hours:** precise, but needs invoicing and multiple agreements.
2. **Service-by-service charges:** set prices for standard work, less record-keeping.
3. **Set fee plus hourly** beyond an agreed threshold: simple to run.
4. **Assets under management:** costs allocated by each entity's value, plus a small margin.

**Question 3: who owns it?** Ownership has two faces. **Legal ownership** is the owner of record, e.g. a trustee who can use, possess and convey property and appears to outsiders as the owner. **Beneficial (equitable) ownership** is the right to the benefits, e.g. trust distributions, without control. A trust can split the two: a PTC trustee votes under fiduciary duty while family members benefit. Decide whether the office aims for profit (perhaps shared with managers as incentive pay) or break-even. Owners get percentages based on their contributions and fund start-up costs proportionally.

Ownership options: individual members; family plus non-family managers or advisors; a complex family trust; a PTC; a standard stock corporation; a non-stock or membership company; non-US limited companies; or a voting trust that keeps voting with family members. Analyse who governs (owners directly, caretakers, outside boards, representatives for minors), the role of trusts, the constitution and the council, outside directors, and whether the next generation votes or only advises.

A **family council** works like a Congress or Parliament: a collective voice that gains value as structures diversify and members multiply. It represents minority stakeholders, holds the institutional memory, enforces governance, and oversees risks: liquid and business investments, legal, tax, reputation and media, and IT and security.

Case: a seven-member family business board (three non-family, four family) deadlocked over direction after a market change. The family members went to the family council, which confirmed the family's goals. That gave the four family directors a mandate, produced a majority vote, led to a vote of no confidence in some non-family directors, and brought in replacements who shared the family vision and brought new expertise.

## Try it
**Choose your office's skeleton.** For your own family (or an imagined one):
1. List the services you need. Mark each in-house or outsourced, and fixed or variable cost.
2. Pick an entity (LLC or C corp) and write two reasons, considering pass-through of expenses, formalities, and any members abroad.
3. Pick a fee model from the four, and test whether every generation would see it as fair.
4. Decide who owns it: individuals, a trust, a PTC or a voting trust. Note who holds legal versus beneficial ownership.
5. Check it against "as simple as possible, but no more simple."

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
   - A tiered holding company with IP Co, Finance Co and Tech Co isolates risks and creditors and can manage taxes and privacy (pp. 95-96, 114).
   - But complexity adds cost, reduces productivity, risks estate-tax inclusion and is hard to pass on (p. 97).
   - "As simple as possible, but no more simple"; start with entities that can be restructured tax-free (p. 97).
   - IP planning depends on whether Mom built the technology for the business (pp. 97-98); child liaisons for venture and the foundation (p. 98).
   - Either answer is fine if justified against the four criteria (p. 97).
2. Strong answer covers:
   - LLC: fewer formalities, flexible management through an Operating Agreement, pass-through taxation so expenses and income flow to the owner (pp. 93-94, 98).
   - A C corp is a separate taxpayer with more formalities and would keep losses (pp. 93, 98).
   - A separate charitable entity: different legal and tax rules (e.g. the foundation payout requirement, p. 108), insulating assets from creditors of other activities (p. 95), and a clear governing purpose.
   - Note the limits of public knowledge; reasoning should follow the chapter's criteria.
3. Strong answer covers:
   - Who pays and the fee model: no more outside fees; costs are carried by family users (p. 98); choose AUM allocation, a set fee or per-service pricing (p. 99).
   - Ownership and control: it must be owned and exclusively controlled by family for the exemption (p. 102; ch. 7).
   - Legal form may need restructuring; the governing instrument is rewritten for family services, compensation and buy-sell terms (p. 94).
   - Fairness across generations (pp. 98-99); profit versus break-even and incentive pay for managers (pp. 99-100).

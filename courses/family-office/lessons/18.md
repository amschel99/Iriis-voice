## The lesson
**Case 3, the most complex system (pp. 215-216).** Three generations and seven households: 14 adults and many children. Extended family uses the office for business deals, insurance and tax. The business has been **sold**. The family started a consolidated investment company for family members *and* office employees. **Six family trusts** now hold large assets and make regular distributions. Generations one and two are financially literate; three and four are still learning. There's a **private foundation** that all households give to and take part in. They have three private banks (two act as custodians), the old accounting firm, lawyers who are secondary trustees, and an independent trusted adviser as primary trustee. Trustees must manage trust costs prudently, including investment advice, family office services and administration.

New wants:
- actively manage its own assets with an independent adviser's support;
- review trust and estate structures for asset preservation;
- co-investment;
- operating the foundation.

Jon Carroll (Family Office Metrics) says there's no single right spending cap on systems. Set the limit by the **loss** (of time, money, people) you suffer when old systems fail. Treat the spend as a **risk management tool**, not a drain.

A complex system needs a properly staffed office playing four roles: **data gatherer, interpreter, protector and thinking partner** (see Chapter 10). Two things to build: the **reporting process** and the **feedback loops**.

**The reporting flowchart (Figure 9.3, pp. 216-217).**
1. **Information arrives** from external providers: banks, custodians, managers, tax correspondence (K-1s), invoices, investment commentaries.
2. **It is sorted and organised.** Technology questions: paper, electronic or PDF? How often (quarterly, monthly, daily)? Can you get raw data?
3. **It is input into office systems.**
4. **It is analysed.** What must I have from the data? What would I like? What is critical to tell the family?
5. **Reports are created.** How often (annual, quarterly, monthly)? Who else reads them, and what do they need? What format and delivery?

The loop runs backwards too. The family decides the content and frequency it wants, which defines the analysis inputs, which defines the technology.

**Data accumulation (pp. 216-220).** Start **internally**. Read the governing documents (partnership agreements, trusts, tax returns) to learn each entity's activities, ownership, withdrawal rights and reporting duties. A multigenerational trust names grantors, trustees, corporate trustees and beneficiaries. All of them get reports. Each beneficiary aged 18+ gets at least a basic performance statement, and each has preferences. Keep the office compliant with these documents and review them periodically with the family.

Storage is moving from paper files to **virtual private networks**. **Edouard Thijssen**, a third-generation member of a Belgian business family at the "cousin consortium" stage, built an online family information hub and founded **TrustedFamily**. It provides secure intranets so dispersed families can meet virtually around the family council, board, foundation or assembly, keeping key documents in one private archive reachable from anywhere, at any time, on any device (VPNs, personal websites, cloud storage).

Technology can mislead. Most office time goes to *gathering* data rather than analysing it, and too much information can disable an office. **Joe Lonsdale** (founder of Addepar) says technology should gather, sort and organise; people with knowledge should analyse. Banks and multi-family offices update their systems constantly, and a family office can't win that race. It must keep re-mapping feeds while keeping presentation **consistent** for the family.

How Case 3 handles each data type:
- **Bank and credit card statements** go to the accountant for review, reconciliation and recording.
- **Investor statements** go into financial reporting.
- **Invoices** are gathered and presented to the family **every other week**, ideally **monthly**, to tame inefficient bill pay.
- **Investment commentaries** are summarised briefly for the regular investment committee.
- **Tax correspondence** always jumps the queue: interpret it, route it, or file it.

**Three categories of reports (pp. 221-222):**
1. **Tax and compliance** (mandatory, low strategic value; quarterly to advisers): tax returns and schedules, partnership statements, the private foundation's 5% distribution check, political contribution monitoring.
2. **Safety and security** (at least quarterly): where assets sit and who can transact or speak for the family. Budget vs actual, balance sheet by location, transactions by vendor and by investment, an annual review of authorised signatories.
3. **Strategic planning and analysis** (the most compelling): multi-year budgets by business and family; 10/5/3/1-year investment reviews with projections; investment expense vs performance by adviser; trust valuation projections for 20, 30 and 50 years; trust and estate projections by generation; tax strategies.

**Financial reporting audiences (pp. 222-223):**
- **trustees** (fiduciary duty: financial, tax and investment reports);
- **beneficiaries** (as the trust allows, plus expense data so they can show trustees their needs for distribution decisions);
- **investment advisers** (regular **cash projections** so they never sell at a bad time);
- **tax advisers** (information during the year, which saves money at filing time);
- **legal and insurance advisers** (annual updates on family changes and big purchases);
- **government and regulators** (filings on time).

Bank and card statements download straight into most systems; build templates once. Custom accounting systems are costly and slow to set up but efficient in the right setting. "Garbage in equals garbage out", so know the purpose first.

**RockIT (p. 224).** Rockefeller & Company's RockIT Solutions was born from two things: **Y2K compliance** and a cobbled set of outdated systems needing manual checks. The key metric is **complexity**: accounts, entities, custodians, relationships. It became a data aggregation and reporting service for single and multi-family offices, wealthy individuals and institutions. It benchmarks across offices, collaborates with banks on data feeds, and frees staff to be strategic advisers rather than data processors. **Infrastructure is driven by complexity, and complexity drives cost.**

**Paul McKibbin** (Family Office Metrics) says complex offices have portfolios across every asset class, geography and strategy, with tiered or interlocking ownership. The **Family Office Systems Architecture chart** (Figure 9.4) layers these:
- investment management (allocation, manager research and due diligence; exchange-traded performance and risk; illiquid investments);
- investment accounting and portfolio construction;
- financial accounting (general ledger, operating company, financial statements, partners' equity);
- reporting silos (performance, exposure, risk for hedge funds and private equity);
- a historic, subject-oriented **data warehouse**;
- **unified reporting** (comprehensive net worth, portfolio-as-a-whole).

**Controls the reports should prove (p. 225):**
- **Cheque signing.** The family has online access and gets cheque and wire reports **directly from the bank**. The risk is reports produced by an accounting system that can be manipulated.
- **Investment transactions.** Detailed transactions in the reports let the family compare activity with expectations. The risk is late or missing reports.

**Archiving (pp. 225-229).** Store meeting summaries, decisions and the reasons behind them in one place, reachable from anywhere. Multi-generational families rely on this history. For Case 3's four new elements:
1. **Managing its own assets.** For example, **capital calls**: route the notice to the right person, know which family partnerships invested and each member's pro rata share, collect the cash and meet the deadline. Keep members liquid.
2. **Trust and estate review.** Produce two annual reports: a **flow-of-funds chart** from the wealth creator to the latest generation, showing where estate taxes hit and where assets land; and a **projection** of how the investment strategy and actual spending grow those assets.
3. **Co-investment.** Pooling, as the Rockefellers and Phipps did, allows bigger deals and shared risk, but every combined item must be allocated for financial and tax reports. The Phipps built Bessemer Trust partly for this; Case 3 needs a smaller system.
4. **The foundation.** Three options:
   - a **donor-advised fund** for roughly **$25M or less** in assets and gifts to 501(c)(3) public charities;
   - **outsourced** philanthropic providers;
   - **build it internally**, best at roughly **$50M+** of philanthropic dollars and when the family runs its own programmes, as long as overhead doesn't eat the gifts.

**Feedback loops (p. 229).** Systems will change. Many families peak somewhere between simple and complex. Constant communication between family, office and outside vendors is what makes the value real.

**Conclusion (pp. 229-230).** Keep the infrastructure as simple as possible without sacrificing control or transparency. Aim for **resilience**, not perfection. The book closes with self-assessment questions:
- Is technology helping or hurting?
- What are the trends and where are we headed?
- What are the biggest security risks?
- What are our document management, storage and distribution issues?
- What separates exceptional operations from average ones?
- Which protocols should be shared with other offices?
- What is the total cost of ownership and the return on new technology?
- How much time and money will we commit, and keep committing?

## Try it
**Map your reporting flow.**
1. List every source that sends you financial information (banks, custodians, managers, K-1 issuers, invoices, letters), with its format and frequency.
2. Sort the reports you have, or need, into the three categories: tax and compliance, safety and security, strategic.
3. For each audience (trustees, beneficiaries, advisers, regulators), write what they need and how often.
4. Draw your own one-page flow-of-funds chart: from the senior generation's estate to the grandchildren, marking where estate tax hits.
5. Answer the closing questions on p. 230. Which one worries you most?

## Questions
1. In Case 3, a private equity capital call arrives for an investment owned through a partnership of five family members, and it's due in 10 days. Walk through how the family office should handle it from notice to payment, and what system support it needs.
2. Real world: Rockefeller & Company's RockIT Solutions grew from fixing Y2K-era systems into a reporting service for other family offices. Why might a family office choose to buy a service like RockIT, or a platform like Addepar, rather than build its own? Use Table 9.1's trade-offs and Lonsdale's "technology gathers, people analyse".
3. Real world: in 2015 Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan announced they would give most of their Facebook shares over their lifetimes through the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative, set up as an LLC rather than a private foundation. Using this lesson's three foundation options (donor-advised fund, outsourcing, building in-house) and the private foundation's 5% distribution rule, explain what a family gains and gives up with each vehicle.

## Answer key
1. Capital call:
   - Route the notice immediately to the responsible person (p. 227).
   - Identify the investing partnership, its five members and each pro rata share.
   - Notify members; collect cash before the deadline; forward it to the investment company.
   - Check in advance that each member has enough liquidity (cash projections) (p. 227; investment advisers need cash projections, p. 223).
   - Systems: entity and ownership mapping, commitment tracking and cash-flow modeling (investment tracking software, p. 214).
   - Archive the record.
2. Buy vs build:
   - Outsourced pros: cost, continuity, up-to-date technology, benchmarking across offices, better people, collaborative bank data feeds (pp. 211, 224).
   - Cons: privacy, canned reports, shared attention (p. 211).
   - An office can't keep up with banks' constant system updates (p. 219).
   - Carroll: invest by the loss you avoid (p. 216).
   - Lonsdale: let tech gather data and staff analyse (p. 219).
   - Decide by complexity and purpose; garbage in, garbage out (pp. 223-224).
3. Foundation vehicles:
   - Donor-advised fund: simple, the sponsor does the reporting, suits smaller amounts (≈$25M) and gifts to public charities; less control (p. 228).
   - Outsourcing: expertise and systems without overhead (pp. 228-229).
   - Building in-house: most flexibility for the family's own programmes; best at ≈$50M+; watch that overhead doesn't eat gifts; must meet compliance such as the 5% distribution (pp. 221, 229).
   - An LLC structure trades the foundation's tax benefits and rules for flexibility (investing, advocacy) and privacy. State this conservatively; it is beyond the book, but the trade-off reasoning applies.

## The lesson
Chapter 9 is co-written by **Robert Kaufold** (Carriage Partners) with Rosplock. It covers the plumbing nobody brags about: operations and information technology. That covers privacy, security, document sharing, reporting, knowledge management and due diligence.

**Family first (pp. 205-207).** Design the operational infrastructure around the family's needs and goals. Then information flows well, risks to the family's human, intellectual and financial capital are guarded, and the family can decide in time. In reality, many offices were set up for one task (accounting, bill paying, taxes or investing), and the infrastructure was an afterthought; form followed function. The real goal is to weave the family's broad objectives into a **flexible** system.

It costs time:
- FOX research: **accounting and reporting take on average 32%** of family office time, anywhere from **10% to 75%**.
- Wharton Global Family Alliance: information aggregation and client reporting ranked **3rd** most important activity in 2007 (behind asset allocation and manager selection) but fell to **7th** in 2011.

So a third of the office's capacity goes to an activity families don't value most, and that's why operations are always under efficiency review. Rhona Vogel (Vogel Consulting) puts it this way: "operations, reporting, and planning are at the heart of most family offices — they just don't want to admit it." Conferences talk about investments, but the days go on account oversight, data feeds, accounting and family-friendly reports. Chapter 3's visioning work is the starting point.

**Assessing complexity (pp. 207-209).** Complexity isn't the same as size. **Wealth only creates the *opportunity* for complexity**; the family decides how complex to be. Complexity isn't evil either. It usually came from good intentions (asset protection, generational transfer, privacy), so understand why it exists and build systems that support it.

The **Complexity Score Sheet (Figure 9.1)** has two parts, and the number of answers drives the score:
- **Family complexity**:
  - generations served; family members using the office; trust beneficiaries;
  - countries of residence;
  - family changes (births, deaths, marriages, divorces) in three years;
  - household staff and service providers for personal property.
- **Functional complexity**:
  - *investment*: asset classes, positions, advisers, monthly transactions;
  - *finance, accounting and tax*: bank and custodian accounts reconciled monthly, banking relationships, monthly cash transactions, tax returns, K-1s;
  - *structure*: ownership structures with employees or non-family members;
  - *entities*: number, how many actively managed, non-investment entities (foundations, farms), locations;
  - *professional services*: law firms, tax and accounting firms, trustees and trusted advisers;
  - *family office staff*: full-time staff and service providers.

The sheet only starts the conversation. It can also reveal **false complexity**. Many positions run by one outsourced CIO with full discretion are simple to oversee, because you only diligence the CIO. But if every position throws off a 1099 or K-1, complexity comes back.

Plot yourself on the **complexity graph (Figure 9.2)**. The horizontal axis is your score (0-10, 10-20, 20+). The vertical axis is how often you report (annual, quarterly, monthly, daily), which tracks generations served (one to four or more). The quadrants are **simple**, **complicated** and **complex**.

**Case 1, the simple system (pp. 209-212).** Two parents and two children; no extended family served. Most wealth is in the operating business, which is starting to pay distributions. The family is financially literate but needs an investment strategy. They have one private bank that is also the custodian, a long-standing accounting firm, a lawyer, and current wills and estate plans.

They need:
- bill pay and liquidity management;
- information fed to the tax adviser;
- investment strategy ideas for assets outside the business;
- transaction execution;
- budgets and planning for future distributions.

**Build it:**
1. **Who does the work, in-house or outsourced?** (Table 9.1)
   - *Internal staff*: pros are privacy, personal attention through a single point of contact, ad hoc strategic reports. Cons are HR cost, turnover, and technology maintenance and costs.
   - *Outsourced*: pros are cost, continuity with good technology, and possibly better people for less. Cons are privacy concerns, canned reports, and a provider juggling many clients.
2. **Use the private bank at little cost.** Get online access and customised views, online wires and ACH so the family can move money from anywhere, and monthly electronic transaction downloads for expense analysis.
3. **Control weakness to avoid.** Don't let the office manager write cheques on family accounts "for convenience" or "in case of emergency". That exposes you to security and privacy breaches, fraud and theft. Instead, give the manager an account holding a **set amount**, which only the family can top up (a few minutes online). That's a check and balance, and the family keeps control.

**Technology for simple systems:** off-the-shelf **QuickBooks** plus **Excel** for ad hoc reports covers general accounting, bill pay, the family balance sheet and transaction detail for the accountant. Combined with the bank's asset reporting, that's often enough. Some families still want custom systems, which are discussed with the complex cases.

**Case 2, the more complex system (pp. 212-215).** Two generations, three households: six adults and four children under 10. Extended family is now asking for help with business deals, insurance reviews and second opinions on tax returns. The business is still the main asset, but the family is weighing offers to sell. They're literate, but the deals are about to get bigger. Same bank, accountant and lawyer; estate and transfer plans done. New wants:
- review the IPS and strategy with an independent investment adviser;
- consider adding custodians;
- make private real estate and private equity investments;
- build a new primary residence.

**Build it:**
- **Hire someone.** A family member running the business can't also run the office, and someone must monitor all the outsourced relationships. Over time the office becomes interpreter, protector, transaction executor and **thinking partner** for life after the sale.
- **Bring accounting and reporting in-house.** With independent advisers, several custodians and private deals, no outside source can give consolidated net worth any more.
- Coordinate the lawyers, accountants and investment advisers on the sale. **Budgets and liquidity projections** matter now, especially with the house build. Complexity and cost always follow construction, so track contracts, permits and costs.

**Technology for the growing system:**
1. **Tax software**: the office gathers and sorts tax information and can enter data directly into the tax adviser's platform. That saves fees by replacing an associate's work, and the office learns the tax picture for strategy.
2. **Investment tracking software**: monitor activity, track private equity commitments and model cash flows. The key is the **format, quality and timing of manager data** and how easily it uploads. Get professional advice; wasted implementations are common. Upkeep and switching costs can be large. Know exactly what reports and information you want first.
- **Outsourcing is real here too.** Dan Berg (RockIT) says success comes from very collaborative provider-family relationships, which free the family to focus on what made the wealth in the first place.

## Notes
- Design operations around family goals; many offices grew task-first (pp. 205-206).
- Accounting and reporting take 32% of office time on average (10-75%) (FOX, p. 206).
- Wharton: aggregation and reporting ranked 3rd in 2007, 7th in 2011 (p. 206).
- Vogel: operations, reporting and planning are the real heart of offices (p. 206).
- Wealth creates the opportunity for complexity; complexity has good reasons behind it (p. 207).
- Complexity Score Sheet: family complexity plus functional complexity (investment, finance/tax, structure, entities, professional services, staff) (pp. 208-209).
- Complexity graph: score vs reporting frequency and generations served; simple, complicated, complex (p. 210).
- Insource vs outsource: privacy and attention vs cost, continuity and technology (Table 9.1, p. 211).
- Never give the office manager open cheque authority; use a capped, family-funded account (pp. 211-212).
- Simple: QuickBooks + Excel + bank reporting (p. 212).
- More complex: hire a leader; bring accounting in-house for consolidated net worth; budgets and liquidity; tax and investment tracking software; consider outsourcing (pp. 212-215).

## Try it
**Score your complexity.** Answer every question on the Complexity Score Sheet (p. 208) for your family, or a client's. Add the family total and the functional total. Then:
1. Plot it on the graph: score vs how often you need reports.
2. Find one place where complexity is **false** (big numbers, simple oversight) and one where it's **hidden** (for example, many K-1s).
3. Decide, function by function (bill pay, accounting, tax, investments, reporting), whether to insource or outsource, using Table 9.1's pros and cons.
4. Check your controls: who can move money? Is there a capped account?

## Questions
1. In Case 2, the family is about to sell the business and build a new house. List the three biggest operational risks in the next 12 months and what the family office should put in place for each.
2. Real world: Kenneth Starr, a New York business manager to wealthy and celebrity clients, pleaded guilty in 2010 to stealing client money he controlled. Which control from this lesson (cheque authority, a capped account the family funds, the family seeing its own bank activity) would have limited the damage, and why does a trusted, likeable manager make the controls *more* necessary, not less?
3. Real world: Archegos traded through several prime brokers at once, and reportedly no single bank saw the whole of its exposure. Translate that into family office terms: why does consolidated reporting across custodians (the reason Case 2 brings accounting in-house) matter for risk, not just convenience?

## Answer key
1. Case 2 risks:
   - Liquidity and cash-flow strain from construction overruns: budgets, liquidity projections, tracking contracts and permits (p. 213).
   - Sale transaction risk: coordinate lawyers, accountants and advisers; act as thinking partner and protector (p. 213).
   - Fragmented data across new custodians and private deals: in-house consolidated reporting, tax and investment tracking software chosen for data format and quality (pp. 213-214).
   - Also: hire a dedicated person to oversee the outsourced relationships; control access to money (pp. 211-213).
2. Kenneth Starr:
   - The book's weakness: the office manager can write cheques "for convenience or emergency" (p. 211).
   - Fix: a capped account the family alone tops up; the family sees transaction reports straight from the bank (pp. 211-212, and p. 225 in Lesson 18).
   - Trust breeds scope creep and blind spots; checks and balances protect both sides (links to the embezzlement case in Chapter 5, p. 114).
3. Consolidated reporting:
   - With several custodians and private investments, no outside party gives the full net worth (p. 213).
   - Concentration and leverage hide in the gaps.
   - The family office's integrator role (Chapter 1) applies; risk is seen in aggregate (pp. 213-215).
   - Good answers mention consistent data feeds and timely reporting.

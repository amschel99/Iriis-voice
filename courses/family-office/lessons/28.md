## The lesson
This morning was financial legacy. Tonight: the values you hand down (social legacy), what you give away (philanthropic legacy), and the family office's role in both.

**Social legacy: four themes** (pp. 340-343).
1. **Stewardship: providing for future generations.** 58% of interviewees, especially inheritors, called it important. One woman's image: money is "like a stream flowing past your house. You can take some out, you can drink it, you can water your garden with it, but you may not dam it up." She sees wealth as everything you have, held in stewardship, and stewardship as "not about the M-word money; it's about the M-word management." Success relies on teaching the young to respect and take responsibility for the fortune. GenSpring's Men and Wealth study agrees: 94% of women and 87% of men feel responsible for family members' financial needs; 56% of men versus 44% of women worry their children won't be ready; 92% want financially independent children; 71% of women versus 55% of men talk to their families openly about money; 90% value financial education; 84% value mentoring heirs.
2. **Value and respect money.** Enjoy it, but don't spend it foolishly. Don't be cheap; know value; splurge now and then, but be prudent. One man: "Legacy is much more than just about money... there is an inherent responsibility." Men and women were strongly aligned here.
3. **Live within your means.** Never live on credit. One woman grew up "macaroni and cheese poor" until her father sold his company when she was 17. Her grandmother's motto, "make it do or do without," still makes her superglue and recover old chairs rather than replace them, and dislike conspicuous consumption.
4. **Wealth doesn't make you different or better.** "Money doesn't mean happiness... it doesn't mean that you are of any more importance than anyone else." If you're not happy without money, it won't make you happy; know your core values first; don't equate love with gifts.

**Philanthropic legacy** (pp. 343-345). 77% of women plan to leave part of their fortune to philanthropy (25% planning $1-5M; 24% unsure). 73% grew up in philanthropic families and 85% are in one now; those whose family of origin was more philanthropic often had family in service work. 72% give as individuals, 55% as a family, 40% through family foundations or funds, versus 17% whose family of origin had a foundation. 43% sit on community boards. Gender differences: donating *time* matters to 88% of women versus 68% of men; *money* to 92% versus 83%; socially responsible investing to 82% versus 40%. In an international study of 200 affluent philanthropists, women gave more for ethical reasons (57% vs 44%) and social responsibility (64% vs 55%); men more for family bonding (19% vs 7%), internal family expectations (15% vs 5%) and public leadership (12% vs 7%). Most didn't expect philanthropy to define their public memory, but saw it as central to the legacy they leave their families. Women see philanthropy as a learning ground for children: leadership, business and communication skills, a worldview and awareness of others.

**Legacy meets philanthropy** (pp. 345-346). Legacy planning moves tangible wealth with intangible purpose. A family vision statement and an **ethical will** say what you want loved ones to *know*, not just *have*. "Philanthropy" combines the Greek *philos* and *anthropos*, the love of humanity; today it means private initiatives for public good. It gives a family an identity: who it *is*, not just what it does. It bridges generations: seniors worry less about not leaving everything to children, and the young discover their passions. **Ellen Remmer** of The Philanthropic Initiative says seniors needn't worry so much; the young take community responsibility seriously. A 2008 Center on Philanthropy at Indiana University study found Millennials as philanthropic as earlier generations: less religious giving, more global impact and social-media activism. Use their energy.

**Meyerhoff.** Baltimore businessman Joseph Meyerhoff started a philanthropy tradition carried by children, grandchildren and great-grandchildren, focused on Baltimore and Israel. His son Harvey "Bud" Meyerhoff was instrumental in building the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum and supporting Johns Hopkins; his daughter Elizabeth Meyerhoff Katz and her children are active too. Descendant Elizabeth Minkin: "This is a business. This is our long-term legacy—to steward this money... involve all generations." Giving makes people happier, shapes how families see wealth and poverty, and brings tax benefits. Vehicles include private foundations, retained life estates, outright gifts, charitable lead trusts and more.

**The Root family case** (pp. 347-349). Preston Root's great-grandfather designed, patented and made the original 6½-ounce Coca-Cola bottle at Root Glass Company, founded in 1900. His grandfather died young in a 1932 plane crash; his father, the only third-generation member, was raised by the founder; Preston is one of six in the fourth generation, with 14 in the fifth and a growing sixth. To them legacy is people and community, not the bottle: archives, photos and films let a child see what her great-grandfather looked like. The foundation builds leadership, governance and education through grant making; a fifth-generation member built an online grant-review system so a dispersed family can weigh in. Core values: **honesty**; **family first** (his father took six to eight weeks off every summer to drive the kids to national parks, and "you cannot replace quantity time with quality time"); **work ethic** (a 9-to-5 father who asked teens what job they'd do; "while you're working, you're not spending money and you're staying out of trouble"). Preston built a 29-year broadcasting career outside the family business.

**How the family office helps** (pp. 349-350). 84% of UHNW individuals feel a duty to give back, and today's families are twice as likely to give through family foundations as earlier generations. Motives mix: tax (estate planners), personal or family mission (creators), learning (next gen). The family office supports philanthropic goals, administers foundations, and supports the long-term vision, governance and committee work. Root even sees his family office as philanthropy: it employs local people.

**Foundations** (pp. 350-351). FOX estimates 95% of family offices support foundations and giving. A National Center for Family Philanthropy, Threshold Group and FOX study of SFOs serving foundations: most manage foundation assets under $50 million (22% above $100 million); 92% have at least one foundation. Family offices help find a strategic focus (55%), develop family leaders (55%), make the young philanthropically minded (58%), perpetuate legacy (67%) and act as effective fiduciaries ensuring legal compliance (67%). Vehicles: private foundations 92%, direct gifts 62%, donor-advised funds 32%, community foundations 24%, supporting organizations 16%, operating foundations 11.4%, corporate giving 8%, giving circles 3%.

**Opportunities and tensions** (pp. 351-352). Benefits: integrated administration, tax and governance; alignment with family values; economies of scale; a single point of contact; meeting the 5% annual minimum distribution; socially responsible investing. Staff who know the family history keep giving true to the donor's intent, run projects, mentor the young and enforce accountability. The tension: the office preserves and grows wealth while the foundation gives it away. Clear family direction, a shared language, clear roles (office: investments, administration, compliance; foundation: grant making, strategy, governance) and open communication turn tension into synergy.

**Mentoring and the council** (pp. 352-353). Claudia Sangster (HarrisMyCFO): use the foundation as a "learning laboratory": reading investment and grantee financials, due diligence, advocacy, working with advisors. Those skills transfer to managing personal wealth. A **family council** (elected in big families, all adults in small ones) sets goals and principles, oversees members' development and dependence, and leads the values and vision work. Patricia Angus says it must be a "live" entity. The office facilitates: meetings, minutes, inclusion, information.

**Conclusion** (pp. 353-354). FOX's four legacies: family (past to future), business (the source), financial (assets beyond the business), philanthropic (giving back). "Legacies of generosity and sharing are often the greatest." The office is integrator, educator, mentor and risk manager. FOX recommends a **Chief Legacy Officer**. Great legacies don't happen by accident: "hope is neither a strategy nor a pathway."

## Try it
Design a "learning laboratory" grant round (Sangster, p. 352) for a young family member, or yourself:
1. Pick a budget (even small) and a cause.
2. Shortlist three nonprofits; read one financial statement each and note one red flag or green flag.
3. Write a half-page recommendation and present it to a parent, mentor or friend as your "council."
4. Six months later, ask what the grant achieved.
Bonus: write your own version of the "stream" metaphor: what may you take, and what must you never dam up?

## Questions
1. Preston Root says "you cannot replace quantity time with quality time." Using his family's values and tools (archives, the online grant-review system, careers outside the business), explain how the Roots turned a fortune into a social legacy. Which tool would you copy first, and why?
2. John D. Rockefeller Jr.'s five sons founded the Rockefeller Brothers Fund in 1940, a separate philanthropy for their generation alongside their father's institutions. Which ideas from this lesson (bridging generations, the next generation's own passions, the family council) does that illustrate, and what risks of having separate generational foundations would you watch for?
3. In 2015 Mark Zuckerberg and Priscilla Chan created the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative as an LLC rather than a private foundation, pledging most of their Facebook shares over their lifetimes. Compare that choice with the vehicles in the National Center for Family Philanthropy data (private foundations, DAFs, direct gifts). What does a family give up and gain by choosing flexibility over the classic foundation?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Legacy as people and heritage, not the product; archives and films connect generations (pp. 347-348).
- Values: honesty, family first, work ethic, community service (pp. 348-349).
- The foundation builds leadership and governance; a next-gen member built the online review system for a dispersed family (pp. 347-348).
- Careers outside the business build capacity; a reasoned pick of one tool.
2. Strong answer covers:
- Philanthropy bridges generations and lets the young pursue their own passions (p. 345); Remmer's point about the young's energy (p. 346).
- A council or separate board gives the new generation a voice (p. 353).
- Risks: drifting from family values or donor intent, duplicated administration, and branch rivalry. A family office can integrate and coordinate (pp. 351-352).
3. Strong answer covers:
- Vehicles: 92% private foundations, 62% direct gifts, 32% DAFs, etc. (p. 351).
- Private foundations carry rules like the 5% minimum distribution and compliance duties the office manages (pp. 351-352).
- An LLC gains flexibility (investing, advocacy, no payout rule) but gives up foundation tax treatment and structure. The student shouldn't overstate tax details.
- Tie to legacy: identity, family involvement and governance still matter whichever vehicle is used (pp. 345, 353).

## The lesson
This morning was about who has the money. Tonight covers where the family office came from, the three roles it plays, and the services it offers.

**Origins (p. 11).** The first family offices probably appeared in Europe, set up by large land-owning families after they sold their land. They were often part of the estate offices of French, British and German nobility in the 19th century or earlier, and their roots go back to after the Crusades (roughly the 1400s–1500s). In the US, family offices followed the huge fortunes of the Industrial Revolution. Family enterprise still drives North America: it makes up about 64% of US GDP and 45% of Canada's, and three in four private-equity deals with private companies involve family companies. Most concentrated family wealth is tied to a family business.

**The Rockefellers (pp. 11–12).**
- **The fortune:** by 1900, John D. Rockefeller's Standard Oil controlled 80% of US oil-refining capacity. After the Supreme Court broke it up, he was the largest shareholder in the successor companies, the forerunners of Exxon, Mobil, Amoco and Chevron. His holdings were worth about $900M by 1913.
- **The office:** in 1882 he started the family office in what is now one room of 30 Rockefeller Plaza. By 2002 it filled three floors, with about 145 employees serving the family and roughly 200 non-family clients. Assets were about $8B in 2003 and $11B in 2004.
- **Giving and passing it on:** he gave away about $530M. He used *primogeniture*: $250M of $274M in oil stock went to his son, John D. Jr., and $24M was split among his daughters.
- **Getting ahead of the tax:** Jr. moved before the 1934 gift tax, setting up generation-skipping trusts of $20M each for his wife and six children.
- **The result:** the family fortune was estimated at over $1.7B in 1987. The Rockefellers created the model for passing wealth across generations: pool the fortune, then plan the transfer.

**Other legendary families (pp. 12–13).** The Mellons, Scripps, Phipps, Lairds, Nortons, Pitcairns and DuPonts founded institutions such as U.S. Trust, Fiduciary Trust, Glenmede, Laird Norton Tyee, Whittier Trust, Northern Trust, Wilmington Trust, Atlantic Trust Pell Rudman, Bessemer Trust and Pitcairn. Their advantage was the same in every case: **pooled, concentrated wealth.**

**Bessemer Trust (Phipps) (p. 13).**
- **Founding:** Henry Phipps, Andrew Carnegie's partner, founded Bessemer in 1907 as a family office with $50M from the sale of Carnegie Steel to J.P. Morgan. It grew to about $1B, shared among 100 descendants by 2004.
- **The cost problem:** by the early 1970s it had 200 staff and cost about 2% of assets a year.
- **The fix:** in 1975 the family hired new managers and opened to outside clients, becoming a multi-client family office. That was a novel idea at the time.
- **Today:** about $40.1B under management in 2003, and now more than 2,000 clients and $57.5B under supervision.

**Pitcairn (pp. 13–14).**
- **The fortune:** John Pitcairn, a Scottish immigrant, co-founded Pittsburgh Plate Glass (now PPG) in 1883. It came to make 65% of US plate glass.
- **The holding office:** his three sons formed the Pitcairn Company, a holding office that kept voting control of PPG. By 1951 there were 61 descendants. PPG paid the holding company over $320M in dividends between 1938 and 1985.
- **Reinvention:** in the late 1980s the family sold nearly everything, including the PPG stock. In 1987 it re-formed as Pitcairn Trust Company, a multi-client office. Every family member was free to leave, and *no one moved any assets out*. It had $2B by 2003.
- **Why it works:** strong governance (a family advisory council), rituals such as meetings and retreats, and free choice to take part. These build cohesion, clear roles, communication and harmony.

**The three key roles (pp. 14–18).**
1. **Keeper and Executor.** It carries out the family's decisions. It handles liquidity across family units and entities, the process for approving transactions, the effect of each transaction on legal structures and taxes, follow-up administration, and archiving legal, compliance, estate, business and tax documents. This role exists at every stage, so trust in the staff is paramount.
2. **Guardian and Confidant** (perhaps the most important). It protects the family. FOX's 2006 "Recasting the Central Role of the Family Office as Risk Manager" cast the office as an early-warning system. Example: an advisor proposes moving from 80/20 to 60/40 equities/bonds. The analysis is sound and fits the investment policy, but nobody consulted the tax advisor, and the tax bill could wipe out the benefit. The office, as integrator, catches that. FOX's critical risks:
   - lack of a shared vision for the future (Chapter 4);
   - lack of an effective decision-making process (Chapter 11);
   - lack of transparent family communication. Roy Williams and Vic Preisser show this is central to keeping wealth across generations (Chapter 9);
   - improper ownership structures (Chapter 5);
   - lack of diversification (Chapter 8);
   - lack of focus on key family risks: reputation, information leaks, marriage, death, birth, divorce (Chapter 6).

   There's also the risk of **isolation**: wealth draws long-lost relatives, charities and old schoolmates. As confidant (consigliere), the office gives unbiased advice and says "no" on the family's behalf, protecting the money while preserving relationships.
3. **The Brain Trust.** Jay Hughes asked an audience who remembered last night's dreams (many hands), a week's (fewer), a month's (a few), and how many of those had become real ideas that made significant wealth (none). The few who do turn dreams into fortunes are the people advisors serve. The office can be a thinking partner that helps family members launch new careers and passions (Chapters 12–13).

**Services (pp. 19–21).** FOX lists six core categories: strategic wealth management, investment planning, trust and estate services, philanthropic giving, family legacy and leadership, and tax and financial planning (Figure 1.2).
- **Strategic wealth management:** governance, boards and councils, constitutions, mission statements, and pooled buying power.
- **Investment:** asset allocation, manager due diligence, investment policy, performance reporting. It can be done in-house or through an outsourced CIO.
- **Recordkeeping and consolidated reporting:** a big draw, because it shows the whole family's assets across accounts and asset classes. Phipps and Pitcairn used pooled assets this way.
- **Tax:** for example, gathering K-1s. Most affluent families file extensions because alternative investments report late.
- **Per-member services:** cash-flow budgets, banking and credit, security, bill pay and concierge services (travel, staff, planes). This also shows who is building and who is draining the family's wealth.
- **Fiduciary oversight of trusts.**
- **Conflict-free advice:** the office isn't selling products, which builds trust.
- **Governance facilitation.**

## Notes
- Earliest family offices: European landed families and noble estate offices; US offices followed Industrial Revolution fortunes (p. 11)
- Family enterprise is about 64% of US GDP and 45% of Canada's; 3 of 4 private-equity deals with private companies involve family firms (p. 11)
- Rockefeller office founded 1882; by 2002 about 145 staff and about 200 outside clients; $11B by 2004; generation-skipping trusts before the 1934 gift tax (pp. 11–12)
- Bessemer: 1907, $50M grew to about $1B for 100 heirs; cost about 2% a year with 200 staff; opened to outside clients in 1975; now 2,000+ clients and $57.5B (p. 13)
- Pitcairn: PPG founded 1883; holding company kept control; Pitcairn Trust Co. in 1987; nobody pulled their assets (pp. 13–14)
- Three roles: Keeper and Executor, Guardian and Confidant, Brain Trust (pp. 14–18)
- FOX's six critical risks: no shared vision, no decision-making process, poor communication, bad ownership structures, no diversification, ignoring family risks; plus isolation (pp. 16–18)
- FOX's six service categories: strategic wealth management, investment planning, trust and estate, philanthropy, family legacy and leadership, tax and financial planning (p. 19)
- Consolidated reporting shows inflows, outflows and who contributes to or drains family wealth (p. 20)
- Value of the office = integration (e.g., a tax check on a rebalance), conflict-free advice, governance (pp. 16, 21)

## Try it
**The three-roles audit.** For a family you know (or yours), write one line each:
- **Keeper:** where are the wills, trusts and tax returns, and who could find them tomorrow?
- **Guardian:** score each of FOX's six risks from 1 (fine) to 5 (exposed). Add a seventh, isolation.
- **Brain trust:** who in the family has an idea that deserves a thinking partner?
Your highest-scoring risk is the first job a family office would take on.

## Questions
1. Both Bessemer (Phipps) and Pitcairn eventually stopped being single family offices and opened to outside clients. What problem was each solving, and what does Pitcairn's "no family member moved any assets" tell you about how it handled the change?
2. In 2021, Archegos Capital, the family office of Bill Hwang, collapsed after highly leveraged bets on a few stocks. Banks lost billions, and Hwang was later convicted of fraud. Which of FOX's critical risks and which of the three roles failed here? What would a true "guardian" office have done differently?
3. The Rockefeller office moved wealth down the generations with generation-skipping trusts set up before the 1934 gift tax, and used primogeniture. Which part of that model would you keep for a family today, and which would you drop?

## Answer key
1.
- Bessemer: by the 1970s, a cost of about 2% of assets with 200 staff was unsustainable. Opening to outside clients spread the costs (p. 13).
- Pitcairn: re-formed as a multi-client trust company in 1987 after selling PPG (p. 14).
- No one leaving shows trust and cohesion, which came from free choice, a family council and rituals (p. 14).
- Lesson: scale and cost push single family offices toward multi-family offices (this links to Chapter 3's trends).

2.
- Risks: lack of diversification and concentration (p. 17); weak decision-making and checks.
- Failed roles: Guardian and Confidant (p. 16), the office as risk manager and early-warning system.
- An integrator should challenge one person's bets and enforce limits and an investment policy.
- Good answers note that an office run by one decision-maker lacks checks (later Chapter 11 governance; Chapter 7 disclosure).
- Keep the facts conservative: highly leveraged and concentrated; banks lost billions.

3.
- Keep: centralizing the fortune, aggregating and leveraging it, planning transfers early, and multi-generational trusts (pp. 12–13).
- Drop: primogeniture. Today most parents want equal distribution (Chapter 11), and favoritism breeds conflict.
- Mention the transparency and communication risk (p. 17) and the office's role in serving all members.

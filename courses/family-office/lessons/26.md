## The lesson
This morning you learned *why* a family bank exists. Tonight is *how* to build one that doesn't blow the family apart.

**Who funds it, and what for** (pp. 313-314). Money usually comes from prior generations, senior members, trusts or a family entity that generates income. Depending on the mission, the next generation can put in some of their own money too. Figure 13.2 shows the family bank as a small slice of a total portfolio (beside the family business, equities, fixed income, alternatives, real estate and cash): a seed investment, not the whole fortune. "Skin in the game" makes the next generation care about the bank's health and governance, so they should help shape its mission and rules. Shareholder and member agreements must cover ownership, reinvestment, distribution, transfer and buy-sell, and can give co-investment rights on each financing. Funding is *not* informal loans, regular gifts or entitlement. It is merit-based under agreed criteria, with discipline, documentation, controls and reporting. The bank is not there to micromanage people's lives. Hughes adds that a borrower should explain how the loan will increase their independence and add to the family's intellectual capital.

**Four principles** (pp. 315-317):
- **Democratize.** Give every member a voice. One well-meaning senior member spent heavily with advisors designing a generous transgenerational structure, and the next generation politely declined to take part. Involve them from the start.
- **Harmonize.** Healthy relationships are paramount. Promote values like integrity, respect, trust and openness, with processes for communication and conflict resolution. Harmony doesn't mean always agreeing; it means managing conflict productively.
- **Customize.** Every family is unique. Decide: a soft bank, a hard bank or a blend? One bank for all descendants, or one per branch? Can the next generation invest? What loan types, limits and expected returns? Do spouses, stepchildren or distant relatives qualify? How detailed should governance be?
- **Professionalize.** Run it like a disciplined family business: formal financing policies, independent non-family board or committee members, and experienced outside mentors. Independent reviewers remove suspicions of favoritism. Even with a board, build family governance first.

**Mini-case: a chemicals entrepreneur funds his family's businesses** (p. 317). He financed two children and his wife to start their own businesses as separate corporations, overseen by an advisory board, and saw his role as raising and educating them, funding them, then getting out of the way. The ventures were doing well, but there was no family bank, no succession or estate planning, and exposure to estate taxes, control fights or bad-debt write-offs. Takeaways: independent advisors strengthen ventures; give each business its own entity; plan tax, trust and estate early; build a professional family bank system with family governance.

**Governance and the forces at play** (pp. 318-320). A professional family bank is a system with subsystems, each with defined duties. Figure 13.3 shows the forces: independent directors, family directors, family shareholders, the family council, and trusts and trustees, all around the bank. Family banks are more flexible than commercial banks because they serve family, not public shareholders. The next generation should be able to design its own mission and governance without one family member, institution or trustee in strict control. Traditional trustee duties lean away from risky ventures, and trustees aren't trained to grow entrepreneurs. The bank can also counterbalance the conservatism of family offices and wealth managers, who worry about capital under management, fees and track records, and fear the career risk of start-ups.

**Mini-case: a family bank without good governance** (pp. 319-320). A patriarch and his spouse formed irrevocable trusts for each child; together they owned a holding company that funded a son's patented invention. The patriarch, also a trustee, started steering management decisions, which hindered the team and caused conflict. There were no independent trustees or board, no policies, no family council and no input from the next generation. The venture was eventually merged into the patriarch's own business, and the frustrated founding son left to build other successful companies. Takeaways: family governance first; trusts for tax and estate purposes, but structure the bank as a corporate entity; coordinate trust and corporate governance with independent members; define everyone's role clearly.

**From simple to complex** (pp. 320-323). The bank should grow with the family:
- **Simplest:** small loans for young members (a first car, summer-job equipment, a small college residence). Basic governance; focus on democratizing, harmonizing and accountability; loans documented to IRS requirements.
- **Next:** larger personal assets (a primary or vacation home). Basic terms, risk policies, eligibility criteria, plus transparency and regular reporting.
- **Entrepreneurial:** funding members' own businesses, with education, relevant experience and detailed business plans. Larger, longer, riskier capital, so add independent oversight (independent board, investment or advisory committee, mentors).
- **Most complex:** multiple ventures, properties, funds or acquisitions; buying out part of the family enterprise or an ESOP; transferring wealth to the next generation. This needs a highly disciplined analysis of capital, criteria, risk appetite and succession, and sophisticated monitoring. Members can co-invest at the bank level and in individual assets; putting in their own money adds accountability.

**Boards, committees and trustees** (pp. 323-324). Independent input helps every family enterprise. Larger banks need a formal board and/or an investment or funding review committee with independent members, clear charters, liability insurance and possibly release/indemnity agreements. Look for: experience with and commitment to helping the next generation; direct experience with private investments and new ventures; understanding of governance in family enterprises; and independence (no conflicts, not paid by family members).

**Structures** (pp. 324-326). Usually an LLC, S corporation or C corporation; in some states a Benefits Corporation; private family trust companies or partnerships are options too. Figure 13.4 shows a family bank with an independent investment committee funding a car, home, business venture, vacation house, short-term cash need or family business. A trust can *hold* the bank for tax and estate purposes, but the bank itself should be a corporate entity under corporate law and the business judgment rule, which is more flexible than trust law and trustee duties.

**The family office, outside experts, tax** (pp. 326-328). The family office, though usually defensive, can coordinate legal, tax and investment expertise, educate members and open doors to entrepreneurs via its private and direct investments. Bring in two kinds of outside experts: an **expert generalist** who knows family bank structures, and a **serial entrepreneur or experienced venture investor** who knows the venture's industry. Every loan must follow the tax code at or above the **Applicable Federal Rate (AFR)**, at arm's length, with promissory notes and collateral pledges, or the IRS may treat it as a gift.

**The Anderson family case** (pp. 328-331). Hank Anderson built a half-billion-dollar Midwest real estate firm. Liam (finance), Ron (deal selection and due diligence) and Eleanor (marketing) joined. In 2006 Hank created a family bank, with Annmarie of the family office as liaison and his advisory board reviewing applications. The siblings applied together. In 2007 the bank funded four properties (two assisted-living facilities, two medical office spaces) for $8 million, a third each. Then came 2008. Hank was consumed saving the main business; projections slipped three to five years; reporting stopped after 2010; the re-amortization due in Q4 2010 never happened. Eleanor stepped back to raise children; Liam and Ron started treating the loan as a gift and fought each other, with Annmarie caught in the middle. Hank avoided holding them accountable for fear of conflict. The family bank was breaking the family apart.

**Conclusion** (p. 331). Start early with education, an entrepreneurial culture and accountable next-generation governance. Customize, democratize, harmonize and professionalize the bank, scale it to its purpose, let it evolve, and guide it with a multidisciplinary family office and independent experts.

- Figure 13.2: the family bank as a small seed slice of the portfolio (p. 314).
- Agreements cover ownership, reinvestment, distribution, transfer and buy-sell; co-investment rights (p. 314).
- Not gifts or entitlement: merit-based, documented, controlled, reported (p. 314).
- Four principles: democratize, harmonize, customize, professionalize (pp. 315-317).
- Figure 13.3 forces: independent directors, family directors, shareholders, family council, trusts and trustees (p. 319).
- Hold the bank in a trust if you like, but structure the bank itself as an LLC or corporation (pp. 319-325).
- Four complexity stages: small loans, personal assets, entrepreneurial ventures, multi-asset and wealth transfer (pp. 320-323).
- Independent members need venture experience, family-enterprise governance knowledge and no conflicts (p. 324).

- Experts: an expert generalist plus a serial entrepreneur or venture investor (p. 327).

- Anderson case: $8M for four properties in 2007; the 2008 crisis; no follow-up; loan seen as a gift; siblings in conflict (pp. 328-331).

## Try it
Draft a one-page charter for your own (real or imagined) family bank:
1. Mission in one sentence, and hard, soft or blended.
2. Who can borrow (spouses? stepchildren?) and the qualifications (education, work experience, business plan).
3. The stage you'd start at (small loans to entrepreneurial ventures) and what would move you up a stage.
4. Who reviews requests: name the independent members you'd want and the skills from p. 324.
5. Repayment and reporting: interest at or above AFR, a reporting schedule, and exactly what happens if a venture misses a payment (written in advance, so nobody has to "hold family accountable" on the fly).

## Questions
1. In the Anderson case, list three governance failures that let the family bank break the family apart, and design the specific rule or structure that would have prevented each.
2. When Dhirubhai Ambani died in 2002 without a clear succession plan, his sons Mukesh and Anil fought publicly until their mother brokered a split of Reliance in 2005. Which of the four family bank principles, and which governance structures from this chapter, would have mattered most for a family whose wealth sits in one shared enterprise?
3. In the Koch family, the brothers who sold their shares in Koch Industries in 1983 spent many years in litigation with Charles and David. What does that suggest about buy-sell and transfer terms, and how would you write those terms into a family bank's member agreements so that exits don't become wars?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- No enforced reporting or covenants: reporting stopped after 2010 and nobody followed up. Fix: required quarterly financials, with the board reviewing (pp. 322-323, 330).
- Accountability avoided out of fear of conflict; the loan drifted into a "gift." Fix: written default and restructuring rules, and independent committee members who enforce them, taking the emotion out (pp. 316-317, 323-324).
- The liaison was overloaded and caught in sibling conflict, with no conflict-resolution process or family governance. Fix: family council and a conflict process (p. 316); clear roles (p. 320).
- Bonus: timing and risk concentration (three siblings, one deal set, a downturn); unclear loan versus gift; AFR documentation (p. 328).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Harmonize (conflict-resolution process) and democratize (a voice for each heir) are central; professionalize with independent directors (pp. 315-317).
- Succession and estate planning done early (chemicals mini-case takeaway, p. 317).
- Clear ownership and buy-sell agreements, plus a family council (pp. 314, 319).
- Note the Ambani split was eventually resolved by dividing the businesses, a form of exit option.
3. Strong answer covers:
- Exits need pre-agreed valuation methods, timing, payment terms and dispute resolution (shareholder and member agreements, p. 314).
- Clear documentation prevents disagreements over what was owed (oldest-son mini-case, p. 312; p. 328).
- Independent oversight and the business judgment rule in a corporate entity (pp. 324-325).
- Harmonize: a conflict-resolution forum before litigation (p. 316).

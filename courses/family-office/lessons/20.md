## The lesson
This morning was about the roles. Tonight is about how to find, choose, pay and manage the people who fill them, including when those people are family.

**Finding talent.** There is no formal training ground for family office professionals. One family office president warned that hiring cheaper, greener talent "may save you on the front end" but "can cost you a lot more on the back end". **Linda Mack** (Mack International) says families often start the search on their own with a clear picture of who they want. Her **360-degree assessment** looks further. She interviews everyone the new hire will work with (family and non-family, inside the office and outside advisors) to define the role and the ideal candidate, and checks that they all agree. Often the family discovers it actually needs a different profile than it assumed.

Mack's key profile is the **expert generalist**. This person has broad knowledge across finance, accounting, investments, trusts and foundations, tax and estate planning, and deep expertise in one or two of them. They act as the family's **quarterback**, coordinating every advisor, with "peripheral vision" to link causes and effects across the family's affairs. They know their limits and call in specialists when needed. They thrive on being the go-to person, and they must be **paid only by the family**, so there's no conflict of interest.

Some families instead want a top investment professional. In the 1980s and 1990s families hired Wall Street people for deal flow. Today many outsource investing to boutique outsourced CIO (OCIO) firms. In-house CIOs are usually hired by offices running more than a billion dollars, at base salaries starting around **$500,000** and going well into the millions. Turnover is low: **58%** of family office CEOs had more than 10 years' tenure (vs. 26% in 2005), and **48%** had over 15 years' experience before joining. The upside is loyalty and deep family knowledge. The downside is narrow industry exposure.

**People business and chemistry.** Shortlist on skills and experience, then interview: family members as well as management must meet the candidates. M.J. Rankin (The Rankin Group) says **culture fit** is often the key. Expertise is a given; chemistry decides success.

The **Norton Family Office** story shows this. **Anne Etheridge** answered a blind ad in the _Los Angeles Times_: "Seeking manager, family office, philanthropy. Send resume to S. Lee", with only a P.O. box address. After a long questionnaire she met **Peter Norton**, whose software boxes with his face on them were stacked in the office. He was selling his company, studying the great industrialists, and wanted a family office; the family foundation was "kitchen table philanthropy". He still had 50 candidates to interview, from more than 250 replies. His questions were odd but clever:
- Do you have a dog, and would you bring it to work? (If so, she wouldn't have to rush home and could work long days.)
- Can you drive a stick shift? (A sign of being enterprising and open to learning; years later she took his Ferrari in for service.)
- Did you grow up on a farm? (A sign of self-reliance. Her answer: no, and she wasn't keen on farm animals.)

A second talk with the Nortons was all about her character, passions and values, not skills. She was hired three hours later and has led the office for 25 years. Why she fit: a service mentality, practicality, and ease with all kinds of people (she had interviewed stars like Bette Davis as a freelance writer).

**Background checks.** Staff will see very private information, so run criminal and credit checks (liens, creditors), use investigators who specialize in financial fraud, and consider drug screening, handwriting analysis or psychological tests (Keirsey Bates, Myers-Briggs). Use profiles late in the process to confirm, not to label.

**Compensation.** "You get what you pay for." The author's pilot analogy: you'd get off a plane if the pilot said he'd only flown a Cessna simulator. IPI's figures:
- CEO base pay was steady: 53% earned $100K to $300K in 2006, 51% in 2011. 31% of CEOs had total pay above $400K in both years.
- CIOs earning more than $400K rose from 17% (2006) to 32% (2011).
- By office size: $1B+ offices pay CEOs about $740,150 and CIOs $367,286. $500M+ offices: CEOs $394,694, CIOs $452,500. $200 to 500M: CEOs $248,636, CIOs $300,000. Under $200M: CEOs $249,300, CIOs $179,714.
- Larger offices use more variable pay. Some see incentive pay as a conflict (it rewards taking more risk); others see performance pay as the only honest measure. The structure must match the family's values.
- Whether the family thinks of the role as an "employee/agent" or a "partner/stakeholder" changes the candidate profile and the pay.
- A typical package: base salary, annual short-term incentives, long-term incentives (LTIs) after 3 to 5 years, and benefits. 75% of offices have an annual incentive or bonus plan (an incentive has preset targets; a bonus is discretionary). LTIs include phantom equity, supplemental deferred compensation, co-investment, carried interest and transaction bonuses. They work as "golden handcuffs".

**Managing talent.** Give every person a written role and job description (scope, authority, where they support others, percentage of time per function) and review it every year. Set **performance goals** and **measurable metrics**. For example, the goal "high client satisfaction" becomes the metric "90%+ satisfaction in a random sample of family clients". Metrics keep everyone aligned, motivate people and reduce stress. Without them, distrust grows and good people leave.

**Talent case.** A patriarch and a recruiter wrote a job description from 360 input, listed the values and traits that fit the family, and set the interview process: three family representatives from different branches plus managers, with the patriarch deciding. References had to include a client of 5+ years, a colleague, and someone who could speak to character. The three finalists: a confident investment expert, a curious relationship-builder who asked how the family liked to work, and a highly credentialed but distant academic. With no consensus, the family had to ask: partner or employee? What will this person actually do each day? Who do they report to? How will we pay them, and how much of the budget goes to talent?

**Family at the helm.** "James", a "recovering attorney", and his brother Mark did direct deals with their father, a trusts and estates lawyer who never intended to have a family office. After their father's sudden death, Mark ran private equity and venture investing while James ran the office and wealth preservation (the IRS paperwork piled up). Lesson: where you start isn't where you end up, and division by skill and passion works.

**Susan Remmer Ryzewic** (EHR Investments, the Remmer family enterprise): know "which hat you are wearing and when"; treat relatives as clients when you're acting as CEO. If working for the family hurts your relationships with them, rethink the role. Family-member pay varies widely. Some seniors take no salary to keep taxable income out of their estates. In offices under $100M, family executives are often paid at or slightly below market, and families sometimes undervalue them, which breeds resentment. Practice empathy, patience and open communication, and serve the whole family, not just the controlling branch.

## Try it
Write a one-page hiring brief for your next key family office hire (or your first):
1. A role description: scope, authority, where they support others, rough percentage of time per function.
2. A mini 360: list everyone they'll work with and one question to ask each ("what must this person be great at?").
3. Three performance goals, each with a measurable metric.
4. A pay sketch: base, annual incentive (targets or discretionary?), and one long-term incentive. Write one sentence on how it fits your family's values.
5. Add one Norton-style question that reveals character, not skills.

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Ask first: do we want a partner or an employee, and what will this person do day to day (p. 251)?
- Chemistry and culture fit are decisive once skills are a given (p. 243); the relationship-builder shows the curiosity about the family that the book values (pp. 250–251).
- The expert generalist/quarterback profile usually fits better than a narrow specialist; investing can be outsourced to an OCIO (pp. 241–242).
- A clear job description and metrics are needed before deciding; the decision belongs to whoever the hire reports to (the patriarch) with family input (pp. 248–251).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Know "which hat you're wearing"; treat relatives as clients when acting as an executive (p. 253).
- Role clarity and clear responsibilities; if relationships suffer, re-evaluate (p. 253).
- Compensation fairness, avoiding resentment over undervalued family executives (p. 254).
- Empathy, patience, communication; serve the whole family, not just the controlling branch (p. 254).
- Pitcairn's strong governance and values work as supports (Chapter 4).
3. Strong answer covers:
- Variable pay can encourage risk-taking; for a preservation-minded family, limit it or tie it to risk-adjusted, long-horizon results (p. 247).
- It must match the family's values and philosophy, or the relationship breaks in volatile markets (p. 247).
- LTIs: carried interest or co-investment for investment roles, phantom equity, deferred compensation as golden handcuffs over 3 to 5 years (p. 248).
- Notes that large offices tend toward more variable pay (p. 247).

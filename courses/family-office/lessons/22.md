## The lesson
This morning you looked at governance of the office. Tonight is governance of the **family itself**: how it makes decisions together, and how to build a system people actually respect.

**Every family already has a decision process.** Some prefer informal ways; others write rules down. Neither is right for everyone, but as a family grows, formal information-sharing and decision-making become necessary just to keep things running. In a Morgan Stanley PWM/Campden study of next-generation inheritors:
- 4 in 10 families have a **family committee** lead decisions.
- 21% have parents deciding alone.
- 24% have parents with the final say after committee input.

Seniors often hold back because they aren't sure the next generation is ready.

**How decision-making evolves across generations:**
- **Generation 1:** the parents decide.
- **Generation 2:** the old practice of **primogeniture** (the eldest son leads) is fading. **95% of mothers and 97% of fathers** want to divide wealth equally among children. That means siblings must decide together: what Ivan Lansberg calls a **sibling partnership**.
- **Generation 3:** a **cousin consortium**, with decisions spread across cousins in several branches. Communication, information exchange and a clearly written process become essential.

Informal decisions work for a founder. They rarely work later.

**Start by diagnosing how decisions are made now.** In many traditional offices the seniors make every major decision and others nod along. Even when it looks like they consult, "everyone knows they need to agree". When the seniors can no longer decide, the younger members aren't ready and may resent years of being left out.

**Three hallmarks of good family governance.** Public companies use two: **transparency** and **accountability**. Families need a third: **participation**. Always involve at least two generations in creating the system. Top-down rules don't last. Many families have signed constitutions, protocols or charters that younger members feel no loyalty to because they had no part in writing them.

**Why the family must build its own system.**
- A system imposed by a patriarch dies with him.
- A system written by an outside advisor has no connection to the family: it goes in a drawer and nothing changes. When asked whether a consultant can just write the constitution, the answer is yes, but it won't give the same result. The content should come from the family; an outsider can facilitate.
- One family member said of their new constitution, "it sounds so familiar . . . it really is ours". That's the goal.
- The process itself teaches the family to make decisions together. If a family doesn't want to stay together, it needs neither a governance system nor a family office.

**How to build it:**
1. Hire an outside advisor to guide and facilitate. A family member can't do it: if the patriarch leads, people defer; if a sibling leads, others resent it; and nobody can facilitate and fully participate at once. Choose someone with real experience of wealthy multi-generational families, not a corporate strategy firm that "could include family governance". The family's non-family office executive or estate attorney may sometimes do it.
2. The advisor holds **private interviews with each family member** to surface the issues people don't share with each other. Purely personal matters are set aside, like a Bill of Rights protecting personal areas.
3. The advisor writes a **report** of the family-wide issues, without names or blame. The family picks which go on the agenda.
4. Hold **formal family meetings**. **84%** of families hold regular ones. Successful families set aside dedicated time instead of tacking meetings onto a wedding or holiday. Most began at the kitchen table; as the family grows, formal meetings become more efficient.

**Family councils.** One early decision is whether to create a council. Size depends on the family. A Gulf family with **400 members** formed a council of **12** representatives. A family with **five** involved members put them all on the council, with branch representation planned for the next generation. About **44%** of family offices have a council, meeting about **four times a year** with about **six** members. It acts like a parliament or congress, or like a board for the family. To succeed, a council needs:
- Family support and participation.
- A clear role, authority and constituency, and an understood relationship with the board, the office and leadership.
- **Action**: a project to work on. Councils without one "lose steam".

Two examples. One council surveyed the family about the enterprise's leadership and direction. The results showed little confidence, and certain leaders and board members stepped down. Another council built cohesion with a family blog for the younger generation and an annual retreat with trust-building activities (ropes and obstacle courses) and education (finance, entrepreneurship, communication).

**Family constitutions.** Like a country's constitution, it sets out which decision-making bodies exist, what each can decide alone, and what needs members' approval. The family writes its own preamble explaining why it exists. Length ranges from **3 to 55 pages**. Three marks of a strong one:
1. **Family input** in drafting. Low involvement means low commitment.
2. **A living document**: reviewed regularly and amended as life changes. One family reads its mission aloud at the start of every meeting.
3. **No need to be complicated**: start with a purpose statement and grow it. One office added norms as events happened, such as a daughter's marriage or a grandson's birth.

Give a committee, advisory group or the council ownership of the project, or momentum is lost. Possible contents: preamble or purpose; membership and responsibilities; vision or mission; values; entities and structures; governance protocols; meetings; the family office board, family council, family business board, investment advisory or asset management board; risk management; philanthropy or foundation board; office governance; family bank and co-invested funds; business ownership; guidelines for ownership, inheritance and succession; education; stock agreements such as buy/sell; employment and compensation policies; gatherings; family history; conflicts of interest.

**Summary.** Good governance (boards, participation, accountability, transparency, councils, constitutions, succession planning) is a compass. It builds unity, bridges leadership from one generation to the next, and keeps the family on course through transitions.

## Try it
Write the first page of your family's constitution:
1. A preamble (3 to 5 sentences): why your family wants to make decisions together.
2. A list of your decision-making bodies (even if one is just "Mum and Dad") and one decision each can make alone.
3. One decision that needs everyone's approval.
4. The next life event that would make you amend it (a marriage, a birth, a sale).
5. Who owns updating it (a person or committee), and when you'll review it.
Then name two people from a younger generation you'd involve. Participation is the third hallmark.

## Questions
1. A family of 30 across three branches has a constitution written entirely by the patriarch's lawyer, and nobody follows it. Using the chapter's process, how would you rebuild their governance, step by step?
2. Real world: Rupert Murdoch tried to change his family trust so that his son Lachlan alone would control the family's media companies after his death, against his other children's interests. In December 2024 a Nevada probate commissioner ruled against him, and the dispute was later reported settled with the other siblings selling their stakes. Using the chapter (participation, constitutions, sibling partnerships), what went wrong, and what governance could have prevented a public fight?
3. Real world: in the Koch family, brothers Bill and Frederick fought Charles and David for years in court after the Koch Industries buyout of their stakes in the 1980s. How do the ideas of sibling partnerships, family councils and buy/sell provisions in a constitution apply to that kind of conflict?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Outsider-written systems end up "in a drawer"; content must come from the family (pp. 271–272).
- Hire an experienced outside facilitator, not a family member (p. 272).
- Private interviews with each member, an anonymous issues report, the family chooses the agenda (pp. 272–273).
- Formal dedicated family meetings; perhaps a council with branch representation and a clear project (pp. 273–274).
- Rewrite the constitution with input from at least two generations; a living document reviewed and amended over time (pp. 271, 275).
2. Strong answer covers:
- A top-down change by the senior generation without the participation of the affected siblings, which violates the participation hallmark (p. 271).
- Moving from a sibling partnership to one-sibling control without agreed rules (p. 270).
- A family-owned constitution or agreed governance (bodies, authority, succession and ownership guidelines, conflict-of-interest and buy/sell provisions) agreed in advance (pp. 274–276).
- Facilitated private interviews and family meetings could have surfaced the conflict early (pp. 272–273).
3. Strong answer covers:
- Sibling partnerships need explicit decision processes and communication (p. 270).
- A council as a forum to air disagreements before they reach court (pp. 273–274).
- Constitution contents: stock agreements such as buy/sell, guidelines for ownership and exit, conflicts of interest (pp. 275–276).
- Participation and transparency to prevent minority branches feeling excluded (p. 271).

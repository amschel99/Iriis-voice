## The lesson
This morning was about identity and honesty. Tonight is about what you actually teach, who teaches it, and how the family office helps.

**Clear expectations.** If you don't set expectations, children create their own. They model what they see: entitled parents raise entitled kids; gracious parents raise gracious ones. A Maserati in the drive and a housekeeper teach a lesson too. When Bill Gates announced in 2006 that he would step back from day-to-day work at Microsoft to focus on his foundation, he said, "I believe with great wealth comes great responsibility." That is the modern meaning of **noblesse oblige**: those who are better off help those who are worse off.

**The cycle of a gift.** Keith Whitaker and Susan Massenzio (Wise Counsel Research, co-authors with Jay Hughes of _The Cycle of a Gift_) describe a life cycle of giving: we start mainly as receivers, then build our own lives, turn to giving back in older age, and become receivers again at the very end. Parents in their "giving back" stage often judge young adult children as selfish for not leading the foundation. But the children are simply young, still mostly receivers. You can teach them about giving, but it's hard to become a great giver before you've made something of your own.

**The Buffetts.** Warren Buffett's children each run charitable foundations set up in the late 1990s. Howard Buffett says his father didn't steer where they give but strongly shaped how: be smart about spending the money. Susie Buffett remembers riding with her mother deep into housing projects. "It was not a check-writing thing. It was her being there." Warren's line on inheritance: leave children "enough money so that they would feel they could do anything, but not so much that they could do nothing."

**Money skills by age** (Joline Godfrey, Independent Means, _Raising Financially Fit Kids_; Table 12.2). It's never too late to start at an earlier stage.
- **Ages 5–8:** count coins and bills, understand what money is for, tell wants from needs, begin an ethical compass.
- **Ages 9–12:** make change, show initiative and entrepreneurial spirit, know costs and earned money, balance a simple checking and savings account.
- **Ages 13–15:** compare prices, understand the link between time and money, earn and start small ventures, commit to savings goals, grasp investing basics, read a bank statement, understand interest, dividends and philanthropy.
- **Ages 16–18:** save, spend and invest actively; link goals to saving; take responsibility for self and others; talk about money and plan; see money as power; read a paycheck and file simple taxes; show growing self-sufficiency.
- **19+:** manage cash flow, run an annual financial plan, carry out saving and investing goals, pay taxes responsibly, give money, time and talent, have productive money conversations, know how to "make a job, not just take a job", understand credit.

**Shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves.** Jay Hughes notes that this proverb exists in cultures worldwide. The cure is individual accountability in every generation. A focus group member put it as "wonder, blunder, dunder": the wonder builds it, the blunder loses it, the dunder squanders what's left. Tom Rogerson (BNY Mellon) describes four generations: **builders**, then **assimilators** (stewards), then the **enjoyment** generation (the "good life" rather than a better life), then the **lamentation** generation looking back in despair. The further heirs get from the creators' experience, the harder they are to prepare.

**The Lyle family case.** Lord Leonard Lyle, grandson of Abram Lyle, was a director, chairman and president of Tate & Lyle. He was also an MP who stood down so Winston Churchill could be elected, and president of the All England Lawn Tennis Association and the PGA. His saying: "You can't just be born with a silver spoon in your mouth" (a joke about Silver Spoon, a rival sugar brand). Each Lyle child gets a trust used **only for education**. Whatever is left goes to charity, because too much money too young "can stop you from trying to go out and better yourself". Sebastian Lyle studied at Edinburgh and Harvard, worked at Barclays and Andersen Consulting (now Accenture), fought for an apprenticeship at Rothschild, qualified as a financial advisor, and founded The Family Office with no family money. He focuses on long-term plans rather than just getting invested, encourages monthly formal budgets (giving, tax, insurance, investing, running the home, luxuries), and uses a few outside fund managers as consultants.

**Inspiring entrepreneurship.** Older parents (50+) want children involved in the family's wealth decisions and also pursuing careers. Younger parents (25 to 49) tend to say "do whatever you want". A study of **entrepreneurial orientation (EO)** in families with an office or business (all at least second generation, 44% third or later) found that each successor generation rated the previous one as more entrepreneurial, and rated itself as less entrepreneurial in the business, in investments and in spirit. Interviews explained why:
- Founders had survived failures and drawdowns and felt resilient.
- Successors felt the burden of not losing what was built, so they lean toward preservation.
- This caution came from deep respect for predecessors, not lack of skill.
- Entrepreneurial behavior isn't simply inherited.

It has to be modeled, taught and inspired: lemonade and vegetable stands, a teenage car wash, and later a family bank (Chapter 13).

**Mentoring.** Bob Danzig grew up moving through foster care. He started as an office boy at the _Albany Times Union_, where a boss told him, "I believe you are full of promise." He went on to the Navy, five years of night school and a Stanford journalism fellowship. Nineteen years after starting he became publisher, then seven years later president of all Hearst Newspapers, leading about 6,000 people for two decades. He credits mentors with a development plan for him. Christin Cardone McClave ("FamilyBizChick"), a third-generation owner and leadership coach: coaching helps people choose the family business **from purpose, not obligation or co-dependence**. Some clients come by choice; others are "voluntold". Coaching is not therapy. Get the "helicopter view", use personality tests and 360s, interview coaches for chemistry, and agree on time and cost. For deeper problems (depression, addiction, anger, isolation, commitment issues), see a psychotherapist who specializes in wealth, such as Thayer Cheatham Willis, from the family behind Georgia-Pacific. McClave says her family's advisors missed a chance to mentor and bridge the generations.

**The family office's role.** Jay Hughes: every member must be educated as an owner of the shared enterprise to take risks together. FOX's conference of 12 offices found five themes:
1. Owners who own their financial education sustain wealth.
2. Owners and advisors share responsibility for an education strategy.
3. Learning must be engaging (entertainment, travel).
4. Fit individual needs and learning styles.
5. Make it planned and ongoing, not sporadic.

Their steps: assess family priorities, vision, goals, strategic and business plans; measure individuals' skills, attitudes and behavior; set yearly goals and get buy-in; build the curriculum, schedule, deliver, evaluate (business and personal impact); follow up (one-on-one coaching); keep collecting feedback.

MFOs use "**edutainment**", such as live theatre case studies by Shaking the Tree where the audience comments during pauses, and Redwoods Initiative (next-gen, women's investing, philanthropy, impact investing). The CICA's 2011 study of 1,200 Canadians aged 16 to 22 found they learn money from parents (83%; 87% said parents started the conversation), financial professionals (33%), the internet (32%), friends (32%), other family (30%), teachers (21%), books (20%) and TV/radio (8%). Only 8% had never sought advice. The top topic was limiting spending. Godfrey's **ten money skills**: save; track money; get paid what you're worth; spend wisely; talk about money; live on a budget; invest; use entrepreneurial spirit; handle credit; use money to change the world.

Her **Big Vision planning tool** is a grid: life stages (childhood 5–12, teens 13–18, young adult 19–25, adulthood 26–45, mid-years 46–65, elders 65+) against self-development, family and parenting, stewardship, and financial competency. Next-generation parents worry most about teaching asset allocation, budgeting and who their advisors are, and fear wealth will complicate relationships or make them targets. **42%** of families used an MFO for children's financial education. Curriculum topics: budgeting, saving, reading financial reports, spending, investing, estate planning, trustee and beneficiary duties, strategic planning, entrepreneurship, philanthropy, wealth transfer, governance, ownership. Examples include Beneficiary Bootcamp and Godfrey's Camp Start Up and Fashion and Finance.

**The Five Ms** (closing with Goethe: treat people as what they ought to be and you help them become it):
1. **Meaning**: what wealth means and doesn't.
2. **Modeling**: kids do what they see.
3. **Mentoring**: guidance, a team of mentors inside and outside the family and office.
4. **Management**: get them engaged; pass the baton gently.
5. **Mastery**: self-empowerment and lifelong learning.

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
- Lyle: funds education (building human capital) but avoids money that blunts drive; a stewardship and "make your own way" ethic (p. 292).
- Buffett: enough to give freedom and opportunity but not idleness; both aim against the enjoyment and lamentation generations (pp. 290–291).
- Cycle of a gift: young heirs are receivers; education trusts suit the receiving stage; giving back comes later (p. 289).
- A personal view with reasons (family values, scale, expectations).
2. Strong answer covers:
- Unclear or changing expectations about control breed conflict; set them early, honestly and age-appropriately (pp. 286, 288).
- The successor-generation burden and desire for independence (p. 294); hostile dependency (lesson 23).
- Five Ms: meaning, mentoring and management (involve heirs in managing before handover) (pp. 301–302).
- Governance (Chapter 11) and a transparent wealth-transfer plan; facts stated conservatively.
3. Strong answer covers:
- Names a real case and states its facts accurately and briefly.
- Links to the EO findings: successors feel the burden and respect founders, so must be encouraged to take risk (p. 294).
- Tools: mentoring (Danzig), coaching from purpose (McClave), early ventures (lemonade stand, car wash), a family bank (Chapter 13), ongoing education (FOX themes) (pp. 294–298).
- Shows how entrepreneurial spirit is modeled and taught, not inherited.

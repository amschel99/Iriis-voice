## The lesson
Welcome to the course. Over 15 days we go through the whole of *The Complete Family Office Handbook*, a chapter a day. This morning we start with the big picture: who has this kind of money, how much it takes to run a family office, and what one actually is.

**Why the family office is a puzzle.** Celebrities and business moguls now set up their own family offices, so the term has become more mainstream. It is still an enigma, though, because the definition changes with whoever you ask (p. 1). The broad idea: a family office prepares family members to manage, keep and grow their wealth *together*, across generations. It also helps them handle the many risks that affluence brings. Its services can include:
- tax, fiduciary and compliance work;
- investment and risk management;
- estate planning and trust administration;
- philanthropy, and financial education for family members;
- governance and wealth-transfer planning.

Rosplock's key point is that the best offices have a *higher purpose*: they bridge generations so the family stays continuous and cohesive around its wealth (p. 1).

**The wealth ladder.** The industry labels people by wealth (p. 2):
- **HNWI (high-net-worth individual):** at least $1 million in investable assets, excluding your main home and collectibles. One report counts about 10 million HNWIs worldwide, up 2.9 million since 2001. At this level a family office isn't on anyone's radar.
- **UHNW (ultra-high-net-worth):** a net worth above $30 million. Wealth-X counts 199,235 UHNW individuals worldwide, worth $27.8 trillion together. That population grew 6.3% in a year (pp. 2–3).
- **The top:** the world's 20 richest people together hold more than $700 billion (p. 2).
- **The biggest group by headcount:** people worth $30–49 million make up 43.1% of all UHNW individuals (Table 1.1).

**What the UHNW profile looks like (p. 4):**
- **Men** are 88% of the group. The typical one is married (95%), has at least a bachelor's degree (87%), is about 58 years old and is self-made (70%). His title is chairman (21.9%) or CEO (14.2%). Average net worth is $138M, mostly made in finance, investments and banking (20%).
- **Women** are 12%, but their average net worth is *higher* at $150M. More than half (53%) inherited their wealth and a third (33%) are self-made. Their most common field is "non-profit and social organizations" (15.2%).

**Trends to know (pp. 4–6):**
- **Resilience.** UHNW wealth fell 1.8% in 2012–13 because of the Eurozone crisis and slowing emerging markets, then recovered. Money has moved toward Germany, Switzerland, the UK, the US, Hong Kong and Singapore.
- **More than one provider.** The ultra-wealthy use several providers instead of one private bank. They are confident managing their own risk capital, but want advisors to watch the liquid part.
- **A gap in the market.** New entrepreneurs want flexible, customizable platforms, which doesn't make economic sense for wealth of $50–100M. That creates demand for "family-office-like" service at that level.
- **Asia.** Self-made Asian wealth is hands-on, takes more risk, and is moving toward investing family-to-family in private businesses. Asia is predicted to overtake Europe's wealth by 2017.
- **Tax, spending and values.** Americans hunt for lower-tax places. Luxury spending is back to pre-2008 levels. The younger generation wants investments that match its values: impact investing, measured by "Return on Impact" and "Return on Mission."

**Billionaires (p. 6).** They are only 1% of the UHNW population but hold 23% of its wealth. The fastest-growing tier is $100–199M, which grew 13%. How many families actually have a family office? Nobody knows precisely, because privacy and self-identification blur the count. The Family Office Exchange (FOX) estimates more than 3,000 US families have one, plus at least twice that many embedded inside private companies (p. 7). Rosplock warns that this figure isn't statistically grounded. The SEC's Family Office Rule now defines a single family office as an entity that gives investment advice to one family; Chapter 7 covers this.

**How much do you need? (pp. 8–9).** This is the eternal debate.
- **Fifteen years ago:** $50–250 million.
- **Now, for a fully built office:** Kathryn McCarthy, who advised the Rockefellers, says $500 million to $1 billion, because returns are low while overhead and compliance costs keep rising.
- **What surveys find:** the Family Wealth Alliance surveyed 34 offices ranging from $42 million to over $1.5 billion. The median was $275M and the mean $516M.
- **Smaller offices:** many exist at $50–200M. McCarthy calls them **"coordinator family offices,"** where most work is outsourced and a family member runs things with a bookkeeper.
- **Barbara Hauser's rule of thumb:** $200–300 million.

The honest answer is that the decision depends on your objectives, time and energy, not a magic number. "If you have seen one family office, you have seen one family office."

**Definition and purpose (pp. 9–11).** The industry definition is "an organization dedicated to serving wealthy individuals and/or families on a diverse range of financial, estate, tax, accounting, and personal family needs." FOX says the best offices support four legacies:
- **Business:** where the wealth came from.
- **Financial:** keeping it secure and managed.
- **Family:** where the family comes from and where it is going.
- **Philanthropic:** giving back.

The office is the family's **quarterback and coach**. It coordinates the lawyers, accountants and advisors, decides when to "pass, rush or kneel," and plans for "a family yet to be born." It also spots interconnected risks. Finally, it helps the family see that wealth is more than money:
- **Jay Hughes:** a family's wealth is *primarily* its human capital (the people) and intellectual capital (what they know), and only *secondarily* its financial capital.
- **Charlie Collier** names four capitals: human, intellectual, social and financial.

## Try it
Place your own family (or a family you know) on the ladder. Write down:
1. Roughly which tier: HNWI, $30–49M, $50–100M, $100M+.
2. Which of the four legacies (business, financial, family, philanthropic) matters most to them today.
3. Whether a full office ($500M+), a coordinator office, or "family-office-like" service at a firm fits best, and why.
Then list the family's capital in Collier's four buckets: name one *human*, one *intellectual*, one *social* and one *financial* asset.

## Questions
1. A founder with $120 million says, "I'm rich, I need a family office like the Rockefellers." Using the thresholds from McCarthy, Hauser and the Family Wealth Alliance, plus McCarthy's "coordinator family office" idea, what would you tell him?
2. Bill Gates' wealth is run through Cascade Investment, a private investment office, alongside the Gates Foundation. Which of FOX's four legacies does each serve? What does Jay Hughes' view of wealth suggest an office like this would also need to look after?
3. Women are only 12% of UHNW individuals but have the higher average net worth, and most of them inherited it. If you were building a family office for a large inherited fortune where daughters and granddaughters will hold most of the wealth, what would you design differently?

## Answer key
1.
- $120M is below McCarthy's $500M–$1B for a fully built office (p. 8) and below Hauser's $200–300M rule of thumb (p. 9).
- The survey range starts at $42M (median $275M), so smaller single family offices do exist (p. 8).
- Suggest a coordinator family office (outsourced, with a lean staff) or a multi-family office (p. 8; Chapter 3).
- Overhead and compliance costs matter more now because returns are low (p. 8).
- In the end it's his decision, based on objectives, time and energy, not a fixed number (p. 9).

2.
- Cascade serves the financial legacy and possibly the business legacy; the foundation serves the philanthropic legacy (p. 9).
- The family legacy (where the family is heading) still needs attention.
- Hughes: human and intellectual capital come first (p. 10), so the office should care about developing the heirs, not only returns.
- A strong answer mentions Collier's social capital and the office's role as coordinator and quarterback (p. 10).

3.
- Use the book's figures: 12% of UHNW individuals, average $150M, 53% inherited (p. 4).
- Put financial education and confidence-building first for inheritors.
- Include their philanthropic and social interests (the non-profit sector is their most common field).
- Plan for multiple generations and involve them in decisions.
- Strong answers tie this to the office's higher purpose of continuity and cohesion (p. 1).

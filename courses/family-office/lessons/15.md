## The lesson
Investing the family's financial capital is often the family office's central *raison d'être*. Rosplock says she has never found two family offices that invest the same way. They do share some recurring **constraints** (p. 172):
1. Family, business, fiduciary and estate planning context shapes the approach.
2. Legal and tax rules inside trusts and other structures limit how money may be invested.
3. Family situations differ. A child with special needs may need lifelong support, unlike a sibling who can earn a living.
4. Underlying assets such as concentrated stock, family businesses, direct investments and partnerships generate or demand cash.
5. Fiduciary duties guide how trust assets are managed.

One fact to remember: by the **third generation, about 90% of assets in US multigenerational families are held in trust** (Jay Hughes, p. 172). The investment approach should rest on stable values and principles, but the process itself keeps changing.

**Concentration: all eggs in one basket (pp. 173-175).** Andrew Carnegie's advice was: "put all your eggs in one basket and then watch that basket." That is how most fortunes are made.
- In the 2013 FOX survey, **67%** of families still owned a significant operating business, making up **15% (25th percentile) to 66% (75th percentile)** of their wealth.
- The great names in Forbes's 2004 "Family Fortunes" list (DuPont, Mellon, Rockefeller, Phipps, Scripps, Bacardi and others) remain impressive collectively. The DuPonts held about $15 billion, yet **no single DuPont made the 2004 Forbes 400**, and none of the 20 family-fortune members did.
- Eight years later, **none of those family names appeared on the 2012 Richest Americans list**.
- Jack Parham (CIO of Eton Advisors) notes that **18 of the top 20** in 2012 got rich from a concentrated business position. Those 20 people drew their wealth from just **12 enterprises**, and **8 of 20** from technology that didn't exist 50 years earlier. Table 8.1 lists Gates, Buffett, Ellison, the Kochs, four Waltons, Bloomberg, Bezos, Adelson, Brin, Page, Soros, three Marses, Ballmer and Allen.

The takeaway: concentration *creates* wealth but rarely *sustains* it across generations. The family office is where risk and reward are traded off through diversification. Moving from one extreme (a single business) to the other (a fully diversified portfolio) is emotionally hard for wealth creators. Help them write down the strategy and commit to it, and expect trepidation and regret along the way.

**Lifestyle vs legacy (pp. 175-177).** Strip it down and every family investment approach is solving for two things:
- **Lifestyle**: the money needed now for spending and obligations. The capital sufficiency analysis from Chapter 6 measures it.
- **Legacy**: what goes to heirs, charity or causes.

Picture the two extremes. One family spends heavily and has no legacy goals. Another, often a modest-living wealth creator, spends little and wants a dynasty, which means a time horizon of **100+ years** instead of 30 to 50. With an "institutional, immortal" horizon, having 10% tied up for 10 to 15 years in private equity is fine. So is sitting through a 10 to 20% bear-market fall lasting six to eight months without selling. Most families sit in between. Supporting children's and grandchildren's gifts and education adds complexity.

**Scale: how much is enough? (pp. 177-179).** Lee Eisenberg's *The Number* asks how big your nest egg must be, and how wealth connects to happiness. Do you fear not having enough? Are you overconfident? Are you living off income or eating into principal?

George Kinder's quick check: add up your investable assets, multiply by **0.04** to estimate sustainable investment income, then add Social Security, pensions, inheritance, home equity and other sources. For families with tens or hundreds of millions, this often shows they can invest differently from ordinary savers.

Professionals use **asset/liability matching**, a pension-fund idea: time the sale of assets so cash arrives when expenses do. If assets fall short of the lifestyle and legacy goals, three choices follow:
1. scale the goals back;
2. re-prioritise them;
3. decide what confidence level and risk you'll accept.

Revisit regularly. Put the less crucial goals at more risk and immunise the critical ones.

**Time: the precious advantage (pp. 179-180).** Thomas Edison said time is the only capital you can't afford to lose. US stocks averaged about **10% a year from 1920 to 2008**, with wild year-to-year swings. Some families pay a high premium to curb volatility. Three truths follow:
1. **No one can time markets.** Taleb (*The Black Swan*): over 30 years the S&P 500 returned about **9.5% a year** excluding dividends. Remove the **50 worst days** and it nearly doubles to **18.2%**. Miss the **50 best days** and you get **under 1%**. Fifty days is just **0.7%** of the period. Stay invested, use dollar-cost averaging, and don't day-trade.
2. **Start early and compound.** **$1,000** at 10% compounded annually for 10 years gives **$2,593.74**. Compounded daily, with **$100 a month** added, it gives **$23,421.66**, nearly nine times more. Einstein supposedly called compound interest the most powerful force in the universe. A 10% straight-line return isn't realistic, but the principle holds.
3. **Patient capital.** Like a family business held through lean years, let money work over the long term. Compare ordinary Americans: aged 55 to 64, they have saved about **$120,000** (or **$135,000** per Boston College's Center for Retirement Research), against a recommended **12 times salary**, which is $400,000 to over $1 million. Families can let time work *for* them.

**The headwinds (pp. 181-182).** Greg Curtis (*Creative Capital*) tells the story of Edith. She inherits **$10 million** and assumes it will compound to **$2.8 billion in 50 years**. It won't, because of five forces:
1. **Variance**: returns aren't straight-line, and volatility has a cost.
2. **Inflation**: about **4.2% a year**.
3. **Taxes**: about **2% a year** of returns.
4. **Investment costs**: advisory, brokerage and account fees, spreads and commissions, up to **2% a year**.
5. **Spending**, planned and unplanned.

Just maintaining wealth takes work.

## Try it
**Your lifestyle-legacy-time worksheet.**
1. Kinder's number: investable assets × 0.04 + other income sources. Compare it with what you actually spend each year.
2. Split your goals into "lifestyle (now)" and "legacy (later)". Give each a time horizon.
3. Run Edith's test. Take today's portfolio and its "naive" return, then subtract about 4.2% inflation, about 2% tax, about 2% costs and your spending rate. What real growth is left?
4. Mark any concentrated position over 15% of net worth. Write one sentence on what would make you diversify it, and when.

## Questions
1. Edith inherits $10 million and expects $2.8 billion in 50 years. Using the five headwinds, explain to her (with rough numbers) why this is a myth, and what she would need to do just to keep her purchasing power intact for her grandchildren.
2. Real world: the Walton family's wealth comes overwhelmingly from Walmart, held largely through the family's holding company (Walton Enterprises) and related trusts. Four Waltons are on Table 8.1. Using Carnegie's maxim and this lesson's argument that concentration creates wealth but rarely sustains it, argue whether the Waltons should diversify heavily or stay concentrated. What would you weigh?
3. Real world: Cornelius Vanderbilt was the richest American of his time, yet within a few generations the family's great fortune had largely been spent and divided, and no descendant remained among the very richest. Which forces from this lesson (headwinds, lifestyle vs legacy, generational division, concentration) best explain it? What would a family office have done differently?

## Answer key
1. Edith:
   - Variance: you can't assume compound straight-line returns (p. 181).
   - Inflation about 4.2%, taxes about 2%, costs up to 2%, plus spending (pp. 181-182). A nominal return of, say, 8-10% leaves roughly zero real growth, or less, after these.
   - To preserve purchasing power: low costs, tax-aware investing, a disciplined spending rate below real return, a diversified long-horizon allocation, and staying invested (Taleb, pp. 179-180).
   - Capital sufficiency analysis and asset/liability matching help (p. 178).
2. Waltons:
   - Concentration built the fortune (Carnegie; Parham's 18 of 20) (p. 173).
   - Risks: the 2004 → 2012 lesson that great family names drop off the lists; fractioning across generations; a single-company shock (pp. 173-174).
   - Arguments for staying concentrated: control, a business advantage, the family's identity and patient capital (p. 180).
   - A balanced answer: keep the control stake but diversify liquid wealth gradually, with a written, committed strategy, and handle the emotional difficulty (p. 175).
   - Weigh lifestyle vs legacy, time horizon, taxes on sales, and family governance.
3. Vanderbilts:
   - Spending and lifestyle overwhelmed legacy; heirs split the fortune (generational mathematics, which the book references on p. 173); inflation and taxes eroded it; there was no coordinated, patient strategy (pp. 175-182).
   - A family office would pool and coordinate wealth (Chapter 1, Phipps/Bessemer), set spending rules tied to capital sufficiency, diversify, write an investment policy, and educate heirs.
   - Credit answers that connect to "staying rich vs getting rich" and wealth preservation (p. 194).

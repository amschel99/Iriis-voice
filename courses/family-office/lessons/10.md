## The lesson
This morning covered form, payment and ownership. Tonight covers the rest of Chapter 5: who manages the office, how it's structured for tax, how it manages risk, and the step-by-step process to set it up.

**Question 4: who manages it, and how?** The governing documents must cover day-to-day management and control. There's one regulatory rule to keep in mind throughout: an office giving investment advice must generally be **"exclusively controlled"** by family members or family entities to qualify for the SEC family office exemption. Otherwise it may have to register. A majority of family decision-makers satisfies this. You design two things and then join them up: the **legal formalities**, and the **scope of services and the people** who deliver them.

*Legal formalities.* A corporation has **stockholders** (owners), **directors** (elected by stockholders) and **officers** (appointed by directors). An LLC has **members and managers**, but can mirror corporate governance for clarity. Stockholders usually don't run day-to-day operations. They elect a **board**, which sets strategy and makes major decisions, and statute still requires stockholder consent for big moves such as dissolution.
- **The board** answers to shareholders and must act in good faith, with the care of an ordinarily prudent person, in the company's best interest. Best practice is to meet at least annually. Its duties: appoint officers and set their pay; review periodic financial statements; check that internal controls work; monitor delegated duties; set stockholder meetings; judge consideration for share issues; approve mergers and asset sales or encumbrances before stockholder votes; keep a registered office and agent; declare dividends; keep the books (usually delegated).
- **Officers** (President/CEO, Vice Presidents, Secretary, Treasurer/CFO) owe care and loyalty. They sign contracts, filings and stock certificates, keep the stock ledger, run operations, and carry out board-delegated and bylaw duties. For example, the President reports to the board. They stay subject to the board and can't make certain major decisions without it.

*Scope of services and people.* Write policies for data security and privacy; family versus non-family employment; separation from the family business; performance incentives; and outsourcing where it's more efficient. In the case family, Mom and Dad want to hand parts of the business and investments to the four children (each managing part of their own money) while staying active in philanthropy with the children's input. Have an **employee handbook** and employment contracts for both family and non-family staff. Family members often expect different treatment, and the structure must plan for that.

*Internal operations questions.* How is power balanced between members and managers (a right to be consulted, or actual decision power)? Who is represented: one member per branch, with equal participation as the starting point? What if a branch has no eligible adult? Each branch can set eligibility criteria and name a **regent**, and decide whether non-family members or spouses can act. How are decisions made: consensus or vote, with a **two-thirds majority** as a possible default and "special decisions" needing unanimity or more than two-thirds? Will there be a chairman or president? How is performance evaluated, by whom and how often? What are the communication standards: meeting frequency (quarterly or monthly), minutes, links to boards, committees, key employees and **audit committees**, and to spouses, children, business directors, trusts and foundations? Should the office also foster the next generation, family harmony, diverse views and social responsibility?

*External operations questions.* Should interests sit in shareholder or LLC agreements, or in voting trusts? Are meetings consultative or decision-making? What information should flow to and from underlying entities, especially those with outside investors? Should the office handle family employment in the business, or members' side ventures and hobbies? Should branches have autonomy through separate shares or "tracking interests"? Should the office monitor members' separate investments, guard the integrity of structures (third-party audits), and appoint external advisers (trustees, managers, accountants, lawyers) and internal ones (facilitators, consultants)?

*Philanthropy, legacy and social capital.* Should the office monitor the foundation's **5% annual payout requirement** or leave it to the family? Recommend donations? Track past grantees' success? Watch the family's reputation (press, community events, litigation)?

*Regulatory and estate cautions.* The office *can* provide investment advice and monitoring (but not for funds serving non-family clients), asset management, tax compliance, accounting, management of outside legal advisors and risk management. It should *not* make **trust distribution decisions**, **trust amendment decisions**, or **trustee or protector selection**, nor certain private trust company services. Leave those to independent third-party advisors to avoid regulatory reporting or estate-tax inclusion.

**Question 5: tax efficiency.** This means complying with every relevant tax (income, estate, gift, employment, withholding, state and local income, real estate, sales and use) and also: calculating net ordinary and capital gain income or losses and using net operating losses; tracking deductible costs such as investment management fees; siting the office and staff for state and local tax; compensation size and form; **joint defense agreements** (family members supporting each other's tax positions on audit, even if one pays more); and **arm's-length pricing**. Cost allocation to underlying entities and how services are classified are key, so draft services agreements carefully.

Going international: the US has income tax treaties ("double tax avoidance" conventions) with almost 70 countries. They're reciprocal, subject to residency and limitation-on-benefits rules, and can cut or remove withholding. It has estate (and sometimes gift) tax treaties with almost 20 countries: "old" treaties are based on where assets sit (situs), "new" ones on residency plus some situs rules for real estate and business property. **Bilateral investment treaties (BITs)** protect assets abroad from nationalisation, expropriation, unfair treatment, confiscation or revolution damage, with international arbitration. These matter when family operations cross civil law (Western Europe, parts of Asia), common law (US, UK, parts of Asia) and Sharia (Middle East). Founders may also manage their AMT exposure through how their share of office income is taxed. If the office reliably earns profits, a founder's partnership interest might qualify as a **profits interest** taxed at long-term capital gains rates.

*Deductibility* (the book's rules at the time of writing):
- **Individuals:** ordinary and necessary expenses for active businesses they materially participate in are fully deductible. Passive losses are deductible only against passive profit. Investment expenses (production of income, managing income-producing property, collecting tax refunds; e.g. financial and tax planning, some legal and accounting fees) are subject to the 2% of AGI floor and nondeductible for AMT. Personal bill paying, personal travel and costs tied to tax-exempt income are nondeductible: minimise or capitalise them.
- **Trusts:** miscellaneous itemized deductions; investment management fees are subject to the 2% floor and AMT; administration costs (tax and legal advice, trustee fees) are generally not subject to the floor; full deduction if the trust materially participates in an active business.
- **C corporations:** ordinary and necessary expenses are fully deductible against business or investment income (mind the passive loss rules), so it's often best to route services through C corps. All charges must be arm's-length.

*State and local tax.* If one of the case children, an avid skier, is choosing between California, Wyoming and Nevada, Wyoming or Nevada wins because neither has an individual or corporate income tax. States can tax only with **nexus**, a minimum connection to the person, property or activity. Owning property or an office in a state usually creates nexus, so choosing where the office is headquartered is part of the structuring.

**Question 6: managing risk.** Risk shows up most in transitions and crises, and touches the family, its investments, its businesses and its social capital: economic and cash-flow risk; tax and legal compliance; employee liability and fraud; property and casualty; privacy, identity theft and loss of confidentiality. *Case:* a trusted employee hired for investment and bookkeeping tasks was promoted over time, then embezzled for **seven years**, hiding it with fraudulent financial statements. The family rebuilt its governance and came back more resilient. A 2012 Wharton Global Family Alliance report (Amit and Liechtenstein) found risk management rose into the top five single family office activities after 2008. Defences:
- **Architecture:** insure what you can; use divisions of labour and checks and balances; multimember LLCs; tiered structures that isolate risk; spreading out who signs checks and keeps books; reputable CPAs and lawyers; internal audits plus third-party GAAP audits.
- **People:** carefully drafted employment agreements and handbooks covering confidentiality, data security and privacy, non-solicitation, non-compete, non-disparagement, domestic worker rights, pay notices and overtime, even for family members.
- **Balance sheet and cash flow:** concentrated stock or a dividend cut can hit lifestyle. A trophy home with heavy carrying costs could force a sale at the worst time if other assets don't throw off enough cash.

**Question 7: the process.** A successful office achieves the family's personal and professional goals, is embraced by family, key staff and any outside participants, is run on time and cost-efficiently year by year, and adapts to long-term change. Set up a board or family council to clarify roles. The six steps:
1. **Mission (strategic plan):** founder and family objectives across investments, business, philanthropy and social goals; goals for the business, the clients and any outside investors; how much extended family is involved, now and after transition.
2. **Transition plan:** when, how and to whom services move; internal people; external effects on investments, business and philanthropy.
3. **Choose the structure:** compare legal forms for tax, business and personal needs, and their impact on ownership, control, operations and transitions.
4. **Establish governance:** ideal management and control short and long term; leaders; communication and meetings; a team for regulatory compliance.
5. **Implementation:** draft and sign all documents, including estate plan changes.
6. **Periodic evaluation:** an agreed action plan, scope, timeline and deliverables; disinterested second opinions; ask whether the structure is efficient, protects and promotes the family's interests, and gets the best from staff and providers.

The authors close by saying that putting family members in leadership roles and on boards alongside non-family members builds buy-in and keeps the enterprise true to family values. Always work with legal counsel.

## Notes
- The SEC exemption needs the office "exclusively controlled" by family; a majority of family decision-makers works (p. 102).
- Corp = stockholders → directors → officers; LLC = members/managers; the board meets at least annually and carries a list of duties (pp. 103-105).
- Policies: data security, family vs non-family employment, separation from the business, incentives, outsourcing; employee handbook (p. 105).
- Internal questions: power balance, branch representation and regents, two-thirds default and special decisions, evaluation, communication, audit committees (pp. 106-107).
- External questions: voting trusts, info flow, branch "tracking interests", advisers (pp. 107-108).
- Foundation 5% payout monitoring (p. 108).
- The office should NOT make trust distribution or amendment decisions or select trustees/protectors (p. 109).
- Tax checklist incl. joint defense agreements and arm's-length pricing (pp. 109-110).
- ~70 income tax treaties, ~20 estate/gift treaties, BITs (pp. 110-111).
- Deductibility rules for individuals, trusts and C corps; often route services through C corps (pp. 111-112).
- Wyoming/Nevada: no individual or corporate income tax; nexus via property/office (pp. 112-113).
- Seven-year embezzlement case; Wharton 2012: risk management in the top five after 2008 (pp. 113-114).
- Risk defences: architecture, people, balance sheet (pp. 114-115).
- Four signs of success; six steps from mission to periodic evaluation (pp. 116-118).

## Try it
**Run a mini risk and control audit.** Answer for your own household or business:
1. Who can sign checks or move money? Is it more than one person? Who reconciles the statements independently?
2. When were the books last checked by an outside CPA?
3. Do people with access to sensitive data have confidentiality terms in writing?
4. Which asset could force a sale if income dropped (a home, a car, a concentrated stock)?
5. Draft your own decision rule: what needs a simple majority, two-thirds, or unanimity?

Using the risk architecture in this chapter, what should a family office's board and officers have required before and after investing with any single manager?

As a board member of a family office, which governance questions from Question 4 (power balance, special decisions needing unanimity, audit committees, outside second opinions in step 6) would you have used to limit that kind of concentration and leverage?

## Answer key
1. Strong answer covers:
   - Split duties: diversify who writes checks and who keeps the books (p. 114).
   - Third-party CPAs plus internal and GAAP audits (p. 114); a board that reviews financial statements and internal controls (p. 104).
   - Employment agreements and a handbook (p. 115); an audit committee (p. 107).
   - Trust built over years leads to unchecked authority; fraudulent statements went unverified (pp. 113-114).
2. Strong answer covers:
   - Independent custody and verification (separating the person who manages from the one who reports); third-party audits (p. 114).
   - A board reviewing controls and statements (p. 104); external advisers appointed by the office (p. 108).
   - Periodic re-evaluation with disinterested second opinions (pp. 117-118).
   - Concentration and cash-flow risk (p. 115); diversify managers.
   - Links to ch. 3's "trust but verify" lesson.
3. Strong answer covers:
   - Balance of power between members and managers; don't let one decision-maker run unchecked (p. 106).
   - Define "special decisions" (e.g. leverage or concentration limits) needing unanimity or a supermajority (p. 106).
   - Audit committees and reporting standards (p. 107); outside independent directors.
   - Concentration and economic risk management (p. 115); step 6 second opinions (pp. 117-118).
   - Note: exclusive family control of an office (p. 102) doesn't remove market, legal and fraud risk; governance must still check the principal.

## The lesson
Chapter 2 asks a simple question: what paths lead a family to a family office? Why are some paths rocky? Why does a family separate its wealth from its operating business? And how does the path link to the family's vision? "If you have heard one family office creation story, you have heard one family office creation story," but the themes repeat (p. 25).

**Barbara Hauser's three typical scenarios.** They depend on the *liquidity event* and the *source of wealth* (p. 26). Rosplock adds two more paths: **family offices through the back door**, created out of necessity and only recognized later, and **investment offices**, which are strategic and built by design. Tonight's lesson covers those two. This morning covers the business-born office.

**Scenario 1: born inside the entrepreneur's operating business (pp. 26–27).** At first, company employees run the family's finances. As wealth grows, taxes, estate planning and investments get more complex and the family wants more privacy. Hauser notes that employees become *compromised and conflicted*: they run the business and the family's personal affairs at the same time. Often nobody notices. The accountant, executive secretary, CFO and bookkeeper all end up "serving two masters."

**The family business shapes the office (p. 27).** In the U.S. Trust/Campden study:
- over 70% said the family's wealth came from the core family business, and 60% still had an operating business;
- 46% of family offices had $100–500M, and 57% were under 10 years old.

The study found an **"operating company effect."** Offices of families who still own a company are larger by assets and staff, younger, serve fewer family members, direct their investing in-house and invest more aggressively. They also have lower cost structures and are expected to be profitable. A private business sets the expectation that the office should perform.

**Why the embedded office is tempting (pp. 27–28):**
1. long-tenured, trusted employees;
2. business structure and cash flow are tied to estate planning, and the company's accountants understand it;
3. the business already holds the records and legal documents;
4. the family thinks it doesn't need a full-time person.

**Why it's dangerous (pp. 28–29).** FOX research argues for separation.
- **Access to secrets.** As roles grow, employees see social security numbers, account codes, balances, house security codes and safe-deposit passwords. That can enable theft, reputational damage, jealousy or even blackmail.
- **Scope creep.** Karen Neal of FOX ("Taking Care of Business," 2011) found risk rises sharply when an employee is also asked to manage family members' personal affairs. At her talk, an advisor told the story of an employee who moved to the family office and, at retirement, **blackmailed the family to stay on the payroll**, "hush money" that happens more often than families admit.
- **Two masters.** It starts with small requests: record keeping, bill pay, filing, dividend payouts. It grows until the employee spends **10–30% or more** of their time on extra tasks. They then fail both the company's benchmarks and the family's expectations, a "lose–lose proposition." Out of loyalty, they find it hard to say "I'm unable to perform this function under my current job description."

**Deliberately separating the office from the business (pp. 29–30).** Some families intentionally set up a separate single family office and move key employees over. The usual drivers are diversifying beyond the business, tax efficiency and asset protection.
- **Example:** a holding company was selling off some subsidiary LLCs to exit certain markets. The businesses didn't need more capital, and senior members wanted liquidity for retirement, gifts and legacy. Putting the proceeds into a family office gave shareholders access to capital and gave seniors stability. It also diversified away from illiquid private stock and created a process for future liquidity events.

**The challenges of separating (p. 30):**
1. **Weak cohesion.** If affinity is weak, it's hard to get shareholders to "double down" on their "familiness" and manage wealth together.
2. **Unequal wealth.** In one family, after a partial sale created several hundred million dollars, two-thirds of the wealth sat in one branch. The "Have Not" branches were further split by dynasty trusts. Their investment needs, lifestyles and legacy plans diverged so much that a shared single family office became a pariah.

**Sudden wealth (pp. 30–31).** An IPO, a large settlement or a golden parachute can trigger a family office. Think of tech founders such as Michael Dell, Pierre Omidyar, Steve Jobs, Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, and Jack Dorsey, many of whom invest through private family offices. In rarer cases a lottery win or mega-divorce does it. Rosplock has seen sudden wealth "rock the foundation" of a modest family. Distant relatives, old neighbors and acquaintances appear, and predatory providers circle. The families that succeed are those who **keep the calls for handouts and the media at bay**.

## Notes
- Hauser: how an office begins depends on the liquidity event and the source of wealth (p. 26)
- Extra paths: through the back door (unintended) and the investment office (by design) (p. 26)
- Embedded office: business employees run family affairs and become conflicted, "serving two masters" (pp. 26–27)
- U.S. Trust/Campden: 70%+ wealth from the family business; 60% still operating; 46% of offices have $100–500M; 57% are under 10 years old (p. 27)
- Operating company effect: larger, younger, in-house and more aggressive investing, lower costs, expected to be profitable (p. 27)
- Four attractions of embedding: trusted staff, linked structures, records on hand, no full-time hire needed (pp. 27–28)
- Risks: sensitive access, scope creep (10–30%+ of time), blackmail/"hush money," lose–lose for employees (pp. 28–29)
- Separation drivers: diversification, tax efficiency, asset protection, liquidity for seniors (p. 29)
- Separation hurdles: weak familiness; unequal branches ("Haves" vs "Have Nots") (p. 30)
- Sudden wealth: IPOs, settlements, golden parachutes; success means keeping handout requests and media at bay (pp. 30–31)

## Try it
**The two-masters test.** Pick a family business you know. List every employee who does anything personal for the owners (bills, taxes, homes, kids' tuition, investments). For each one, estimate the % of their time it takes, and mark every piece of sensitive data they can reach. Anyone above 10% is in Karen Neal's danger zone. Write the first step you'd take to separate that work into a family office.

## Questions
1. A family business's long-serving CFO now also pays the owners' personal bills, manages their investment accounts and holds their safe-deposit passwords. Using the chapter's risks (two masters, scope creep, access to secrets, the blackmail story), explain to the family why and how to separate this work.
2. In 2014 Facebook bought WhatsApp for about $19 billion, turning its founders into billionaires almost overnight. Which path is this (Hauser's scenarios, sudden wealth, investment office)? What does the chapter say such founders must guard against in the first years?
3. The Wrigley family sold its company to Mars in 2008. Using the "Haves and Have Nots" example and the separation challenges on p. 30, what would make it hard for a large business-owning family to manage the proceeds through one shared family office after a sale like this?

## Answer key
1.
- Two masters and conflict of interest (Hauser, pp. 26–27).
- Scope creep: 10–30%+ of time, failing both jobs, "lose–lose" (p. 29).
- Sensitive data enables theft, reputational damage and blackmail; the "hush money" story (p. 28).
- Fix: a separate legal entity and office location with its own staff (FOX "Boundaries That Matter").
- Move the CFO deliberately, with a new job description (pp. 29–30).

2.
- Sudden wealth from a liquidity event, similar to the tech founders the chapter lists (pp. 30–31).
- Could lead to an investment office if the founder wants to grow wealth from wealth (p. 31, tonight's lesson).
- Guard against handout requests, distant relatives, media and predatory providers (p. 31).
- Kessler's "Park It" advice (tonight's lesson): don't rush to deploy the money.

3.
- Branches may hold very different shares of the wealth; trusts fragment it further (p. 30).
- Investment needs, lifestyles and legacy plans diverge, so one office may not serve all branches.
- Weak cohesion and familiness make "doubling down" hard (p. 30).
- Options: different offices per branch (the Kessler model) or a multi-family office.
- Keep facts conservative: only the sale itself is assumed, not the family's internal details.

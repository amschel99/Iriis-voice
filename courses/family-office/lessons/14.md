## The lesson
Being exempt from registration doesn't make you invisible. Tonight covers the disclosure rules that still reach family offices, three insider trading cases, and how to build compliance and record-keeping. The Smith Family Office structure from this morning is the worked example throughout.

**1. Beneficial ownership of public companies: Schedules 13D and 13G (pp. 149-151).** If you acquire **more than 5%** of a class of a public company's equity, you must file within **10 days**. Day one is the first calendar day after the trade date. A *beneficial owner* is anyone who, directly or indirectly, has or shares the power to **vote** the security or to **dispose** of it. The long form is **Schedule 13D**. You may use the short **Schedule 13G** if you qualify under one of these exemptions:
- **Ordinary course of business.** The securities were bought in the ordinary course, not to change or influence control. Only certain filers can use this, so ask counsel whether family office clients can.
- **Passive investor.** You have no control intent, you aren't using another 13G exemption, and you own **less than 20%**. If you cross 20% or stop being passive, you switch to 13D.
- **Other acquisitions (exempt investors).** These file a 13G shortly after year-end. This applies when the purchase came through a registered offering, or when the purchase plus the past 12 months' purchases don't exceed **2%** of the class.

**2. Insiders: Section 16, Forms 3, 4 and 5 (pp. 151-152).** **Directors, executive officers and holders of more than 10%** of a company's registered equity must report their holdings and every change. The presumption is that they know inside information. There are two tests:
- To decide *whether* you are a more-than-10% holder, use the voting or disposition-power test.
- To decide *which* securities you report, use **pecuniary interest**: the right to profit from them.

The deadlines:
- **Form 3** within 10 days of becoming an insider.
- **Form 4** within **2 business days** of a change. This is a famously short window, so warn your family.
- **Form 5** within 45 days after the company's fiscal year-end, unless there was nothing to report or everything was already filed on Form 4.

**3. Multi-tier structures (pp. 152-154).** Anyone who directly or indirectly controls an entity that owns the shares may be a beneficial owner. There's no bright-line test; it depends on the facts.

In the Smith example, InvestCo B buys **12%** of an Issuer. That is under 20% and passive, so a **13G** is allowed. InvestCo B, IntermediaryCo and Family OfficeCo must all be named. TrustCo and The Smith Trust *might* be left out, because Family OfficeCo invests with full discretion and they play no role in the decisions. Entities under **common control must aggregate**: InvestCo A's 2% plus B's 12% makes **14%**.

**4. Institutional investment managers: Form 13F (pp. 154-157).** You are one if you exercise discretion over **$100 million or more of Section 13(f) securities**. Those are exchange-traded equities (including Nasdaq NMS), certain options and warrants, closed-end funds including ETFs, and some convertible debt. Open-end mutual funds don't count, and neither do shares traded only on non-US exchanges such as Toronto, the FTSE or the Nikkei, or pink-sheet ADRs. Use the SEC's quarterly official list.

How the timing works:
- Test the value on the **last trading day of each month**, using the trade date.
- The first report covers the **December quarter of the year you crossed $100M**, due 45 days after quarter-end.
- You then file for March, June and September of the next year even if you dropped below the threshold.

Who reports: a parent shares discretion with its subsidiaries. The ultimate parent can file and list the subsidiaries as "Other Included Managers", and they then make notice filings.

In the Smith example, InvestCo B holds $150 million on October 31. IntermediaryCo and Family OfficeCo are control persons and are included. TrustCo and the Trust may again be excluded because the office has discretion.

**5. Large traders: Form 13H (pp. 157-159).** A large trader exercises discretion, directly or through entities it controls, over accounts trading US NMS securities of **2 million shares or $20 million in a day**, or **20 million shares or $200 million in a month**. Control is presumed at **25%** of voting securities, or 25% of partnership capital. All affiliated trading is aggregated.

In the Smith example, InvestCo A trades $200 million in October and B trades only $5 million. They are under common control, so B is caught too. Either both file, or IntermediaryCo files once for both.

**6. Insider trading (pp. 159-162).** The SEC must show:
- **material non-public information**, judged with hindsight: would an investor consider it important?
- **a duty to the company**, which can pass through family or social ties from a fiduciary (the tipper) to a tippee;
- **scienter**: acting knowingly or recklessly.

Three cases from the book:
- **SEC v. Goetz (2011).** An attorney traded on deal documents his attorney daughter brought home for the holidays. He disgorged his profits and paid an equal penalty. Lesson: guard documents even at home.
- **SEC v. Deskovick and Haig (2011).** A bank director told a friend about a planned sale, and the friend told Haig, who traded. *Both* Deskovick (who never traded) and Haig paid penalties, and Deskovick was barred as a public-company officer or director for five years.
- **SEC v. Macdonald and Maresca (2010).** Profits of $8,198, $12,335 and $25,508 still drew enforcement. The size of the profit is irrelevant.

**7. Compliance policy (pp. 162-163).** A registered adviser must have written policies and a **chief compliance officer (CCO)**. Unregistered offices should copy the practice. It guards against fraud and misappropriation, staff liability, reputational harm and directors' duty failures, and it mitigates SEC sanctions. The components are:
- a code of ethics, including personal trading;
- policies against misuse of material non-public information;
- current and accurate books and records;
- business continuity plans for disasters or the loss of key people;
- a privacy policy, given to natural-person clients every year;
- anti-money-laundering procedures, updated every year;
- marketing restrictions.

**8. Document retention (pp. 163-165).** Registered advisers keep corporate, accounting, agreement and communication records for **2 years in the office plus 3 more years** easily accessible. Articles, minute books and stock books are kept until **3 years after the enterprise ends**. Electronic records must be indexed and duplicated, and you must be able to produce them promptly. A good policy:
- defines records broadly, electronic included;
- appoints a record owner;
- has everyone follow it;
- stores records safely, in multiple locations;
- destroys records strictly by the policy, never around an audit or lawsuit.

**9. In-house or outsourced CCO (p. 166).** SEC staff doubt that an outsourced CCO serving many firms, far away, without real authority, can be effective.

**10. Special issues (pp. 166-167).** There is no *de minimis* exception: a small slip can force registration. Families sometimes hold mutual funds rather than ETFs to stay out of 13F, and they can ask for confidential treatment. Expect regulators to stay vigilant and keep closing loopholes.

## Notes
- 13D/13G: more than 5% of a class, file within 10 days (day one is the day after the trade) (p. 149).
- 13G is allowed if: ordinary course (certain filers), passive and under 20%, or exempt purchases (registered offering, or 2% or less in 12 months) filed at year-end (pp. 150-151).
- Section 16 insiders: directors, executive officers, over-10% holders; Form 3 in 10 days, Form 4 in 2 business days, Form 5 within 45 days of fiscal year-end (pp. 151-152).
- Two tests: voting or disposition power decides insider status; pecuniary interest decides what you report (p. 152).
- Multi-tier: indirect control means beneficial ownership; common-control entities aggregate (Smith: 2% + 12% = 14%) (pp. 152-154).
- 13F: discretion over $100M+ of 13(f) securities; mutual funds and foreign-only shares excluded; tested monthly; first report for the December quarter (pp. 154-156).
- 13H large trader: 2M shares or $20M a day, 20M shares or $200M a month; 25% control presumption; aggregate affiliates (pp. 157-159).
- Insider trading needs material non-public information, a duty (passes tipper to tippee) and scienter; cases: Goetz, Deskovick/Haig, Macdonald/Maresca (pp. 159-162).
- Compliance policy: ethics code, non-public information policy, books and records, continuity, privacy, AML, marketing; CCO (pp. 162-163).
- Records: 2 years in office + 3 accessible; organisational books until 3 years after termination (p. 164).
- No de minimis exception; ask for confidential 13F treatment (pp. 166-167).

## Try it
**Disclosure decision drill.** Take one position in a family portfolio, real or imagined (for example, 7% of a listed company held by a family LLC):
1. Is it over 5%? If yes, decide 13D or 13G. Do you have any control intent? Is it under 20%?
2. Is anyone in the family a director or officer, or is the holding over 10%? That brings in Forms 3, 4 and 5.
3. Add up every 13(f) security the office directs at month-end. Is it $100M or more? That means a 13F.
4. Add up the busiest day's and month's trading across all affiliated entities against the 13H thresholds.
5. Draw your structure and mark who must be named in each report, and who can argue they have no discretion.
Then write a one-page "home rules" note for your family on handling confidential company information.

## Questions
1. The Smiths' InvestCo B owns 12% of an Issuer, and InvestCo A owns 2%. Jennifer now wants to push for two board seats. Which form must they file now, who must be named, and why does aggregation matter?
2. Real world: Archegos (Bill Hwang's family office) built enormous exposures to a few stocks through total return swaps held with several banks rather than owning the shares directly. Using tonight's rules (beneficial ownership means voting or disposition power, and 13F counts 13(f) securities), explain why such positions could stay out of 13D/13G and 13F filings. What does this tell a family office CIO about the gap between "what we must disclose" and "what our risk really is"?
3. Real world: Rajat Gupta, a Goldman Sachs director, was convicted in 2012 of passing boardroom information to the hedge fund manager Raj Rajaratnam, who traded on it. Compare this with SEC v. Deskovick and Haig. What duty did each tipper break? What policies from tonight's compliance list would you put in a family office whose members sit on public company boards?

## Answer key
1. Board seats:
   - Seeking board seats is intent to influence control, so the passive 13G exemption is lost and they must file a 13D (pp. 150, 153).
   - Those named include InvestCo B, IntermediaryCo and Family OfficeCo; whether TrustCo and the Trust are included depends on discretion and control (p. 153).
   - Common control aggregates A and B: 14% (p. 154). Aggregation also matters for the 20% and 10% (Section 16) thresholds.
   - If a Smith family member joins the board, they become a director and a Section 16 insider (Forms 3, 4, 5) (pp. 151-152).
2. Archegos:
   - Swaps give economic exposure without voting or disposition power over the shares, so they may not count as beneficial ownership; swaps aren't 13(f) securities either (pp. 149, 154).
   - The banks, not Archegos, held the hedging shares.
   - The lesson: the disclosure rules monitor systemic risk but don't capture every exposure. A CIO should track total economic exposure and leverage across all counterparties, which ties to the risk management duty in Chapter 8 (pp. 196).
   - Note that rules have since evolved; the book is from 2014.
3. Gupta and Deskovick:
   - Both were directors with a fiduciary duty to their company; each passed material non-public information on, and the tippees traded (pp. 160-161).
   - Deskovick was sanctioned even though she didn't trade herself; she was barred as an officer or director for five years.
   - Recommended policies: an ethics code with personal trading rules; a policy on misusing non-public information; restricted lists; pre-clearance for trades in companies where family members sit on boards; training; and safeguarding documents at home (Goetz) (pp. 161-163).
   - Also cover Form 4 discipline: 2 business days (p. 152).

## The lesson
Chapter 11 (Barbara Hauser and Kirby Rosplock) covers governance: how decisions get made. It opens with Jay Hughes: the great political philosophers understood that politics is only about **joint decision making**, which is why Aristotle said "the foundation of any government is the family". Countries have governance systems and so do families. Dictators risk being overthrown; elections and constitutions are respected by the people who made them. A family office can do a lot to keep a family together through good decision-making procedures. Tonight's lesson covers governance of the family itself. This morning is about governance of the office.

**Run the office like a business.** Most traditional SFOs focus on overseeing investments, not on corporate governance. The hallmarks of good corporate governance are **transparency and accountability** (see the OECD principles). How complex this gets depends on the stage. A first-generation office serving a matriarch or patriarch is simple. Once the second generation (a group of siblings) takes over, the office answers to people with different interests, and you need policies, not ad hoc decisions or favoritism that breed tension and resentment.

**The board.** The standard sign of good governance is a board that is good and involved. Less than half of families with a family office (48%) have an advisory board or board of directors, and fewer have an effective or independent one. Legally the board directs the company, and some blame the 2008 crisis on inattentive boards at financial institutions. The OECD gives the board **eight functions**:
1. Review and guide strategy, major plans, risk policy, budgets and business plans; set performance objectives; monitor; oversee major spending, acquisitions and divestitures.
2. Monitor how well governance works and change it as needed.
3. Select, pay, monitor and, when necessary, replace key executives; oversee succession.
4. Align executive and board pay with long-term interests.
5. Make board nomination and election formal and transparent.
6. Manage conflicts of interest, including misuse of assets and related-party deals.
7. Ensure the integrity of accounting and reporting (independent audit, risk controls, compliance).
8. Oversee disclosure and communication.

A key board job is hiring and firing the CEO. In many offices the CEO grew from an advisor relationship with the seniors, not from board selection, which makes them hard to confront or replace. A proper board is elected by the office's owners, with terms and duties in the bylaws. Use an odd number to avoid deadlocks: **five to seven** for an SFO. Meet at least quarterly, with written agendas and minutes.

**Independent directors.** Professor John Ward recommends at least **three** independent directors for family businesses. In practice family office boards average **four family members and one outsider**. Aim to add more over time, and pick people who know the SFO world. In one family, a new independent director immediately went after "loss assets" like the yacht, without understanding what it meant to the family.

**Advisory board as a step.** If sharing financial information with outsiders feels too risky, start with an **advisory board**. Candidates are usually flattered to join, and it shows the family how comfortable it is sharing information before forming a full board.

**Accountability to the family.** Office managers often grew into their roles serving seniors who preferred not to share much with younger members. Over time that fails. Transparency has to extend to all family members. Shielding heirs from the responsibilities of ownership disempowers them.

**Pricing.** Offices are often started and funded by the seniors, and the next generation may not want to keep funding them at the same level. In one family the seniors paid all the second generation's office costs. The non-family executive worried this bred entitlement and later resentment once heirs faced the real cost. A suggestion: a **scaled fee-sharing arrangement** that increases over the years and doubles as a succession plan for ownership. The board should take this up early.

**Long-term strategy** needs a clear baseline (Chapter 6) and a vision two or three generations out (Chapter 4), then a long-range plan.

**The next generation.** In a study, **31%** of next-generation participants felt their family's governance was weak and only **30%** were largely or completely satisfied. Participation grows with age: only 40-year-olds had a full voice (25%), plus 33% with strong input. Seniors worry whether heirs can handle wealth ("shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves in three generations"). The board can back education: outside programs, a custom series (one family trains its next generation to become trustees), and the family foundation, with roles matched to each person's talents and time.

**Succession of executives and of the office.** **47%** of family offices have no succession plan (U.S. Trust/Campden). Why it's hard: leaders must face their own mortality, and for founders the office is part of who they are. They struggle to picture who will lead and how. And a transition is a series of steps, often with overlap, not one event. Too many are sudden, on-the-fly transitions.

**Alice's story** shows the cost. Her father called her in the night with his lawyer, a stack of documents and a fountain pen. She got power of attorney, an envelope of keys, and responsibility for the family businesses and wealth. She had an MBA and a law degree but had never worked there or been prepared. He died days later. She surrounded herself with his advisors, reviewed every entity, and talked with her siblings. None wanted to run the businesses, so over about 10 years all dozen were sold, and the office expanded to serve the larger liquid wealth. It ended well, but with a lot of heartache. She suspects his pride and love of the work kept him from bringing her in sooner.

What works:
- Treat leadership change as normal and build it into the strategic plan.
- Move the outgoing president/CEO to **chairman** for a year or so, as mentor and sounding board, not as a shadow CEO.
- Always have a **contingency leadership plan**. A Midwest peer group of family office executives agreed to step in for one another if a leader became incapacitated.
- Use a **bridging non-family executive** when the next generation isn't ready or interested.

**The Winchester family case.** They fled Nazi-occupied Europe, rebuilt in the US under patriarch James, and grew a fortune in the hundreds of millions: four branches, 15 family members, 150+ accounts. Son Raymond worked beside James for about five years (bringing ideas like a stock ticker) and took over when James died. He has held the post for over 50 years. His philosophy: "The family's wealth is a stream from which you drink but not a lake from which you empty out." About 15 years ago they planned for succession. Son-in-law **Jeffrey** saw that the wealth was fragmenting, so to stay viable for 20 to 30 years they would need new wealth, outside families' assets, or both. After a strategic review (consultants, family surveys) they **partnered with an MFO**. The MFO gained credibility in the region. The family got research, due diligence and education resources and could hire a non-family CEO. Raymond became chairman; Jeffrey built relationships. Continuity of values and advisors was kept.

## Try it
Run a board health check on your family office (or plan one):
1. Score each of the eight OECD functions 0 (nobody does it), 1 (informally), 2 (formally, documented).
2. Count the board: size, family vs. independent members, meeting frequency, minutes?
3. Write a one-paragraph contingency plan: if the office leader were unavailable tomorrow, who steps in, who decides, and where are the keys, passwords and documents?
4. Pick one function scoring 0 and assign an owner and a date.

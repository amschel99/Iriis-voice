## The lesson
Final lesson. We finish the world tour and pull the whole course together.

**Latin America** (pp. 364-366). After a decade of growth on strong commodity prices and international credit, the engine stalled. Latin America was the only region whose UHNW population and wealth *fell*: down 4.1% to 14,150 people, driven by Brazil. Excluding Brazil, the region would have ticked up. Growth varied: Argentina 6.7%, Ecuador 6%, Paraguay 6.1%, Costa Rica 17.6%, Panama 9.5%. Challenges include rigid labor markets, trade protectionism and political uncertainty. Brazil posted its largest-ever trade deficit (over $5.3 billion in the first five months of 2013), and GDP fell amid civil unrest, yet its 50 billionaires hold a third of its UHNW wealth. It has 4,000+ UHNW individuals; one in ten does business mainly abroad, and nearly as many own homes abroad.

Iñigo Susaeta Córdoba (Arcano Family Office, Spain and the U.S.): families now live everywhere, with children and cousins across the world, but family ties stay strong, so the MFO must serve the matriarch in Spain and the children in Chile, Argentina or the U.S. at the same level. First-generation wealth creators drive demand, but talent and political stability are uneven, so many use MFOs for multi-jurisdictional planning. MFOs are still uncommon because household net worth is lower and families are used to their own teams. SFOs reflect new wealth (less multi-generational planning), the U.S. model and Spanish cultural roots. **Security is paramount**: kidnapping, extortion and blackmail are real threats. One Colombian family had an elder kidnapped and held for ransom for weeks; with help from other powerful families they negotiated his release unharmed. One branch then opened a second family office in Miami for diversification and for members no longer living in Colombia, who now visit in armored cars and private planes with ex-military security. Gateway cities offer a different kind of anonymity.

**Europe** (pp. 366-370). One of the oldest bastions of wealth, dating back to the landed estates of the Crusades era. There are an estimated 1,000 family offices, including private offices for the Albas (Spain's eldest aristocratic family), Sainsburys, Pears and Guinnesses. Spain has many newly powerful families and about 100 SFOs, but only 7 manage €1 billion, versus about 80 across Europe. Europe has 58,065 UHNW people worth $7.675 trillion, up 8.7%, buoyed by German and Swiss stocks; Greece is now the second-fastest-growing UHNW population in Europe. London has the most UHNW people (6,360, up 5.7%). Wealth is flowing from peripheral economies to the core (Germany, Switzerland, the UK), and non-domiciled UHNW individuals are nearly a fifth of Switzerland's and almost a third of the UK's.
- **Germany**, Europe's largest economy, narrowly avoided recession (+0.1% GDP) and leads the euro rescue, amid some resentment. The IMF's Subir Lall flagged bank vulnerabilities. Consumer and luxury spending stayed strong, including Chinese and Russian tourists.
- **Switzerland** is the traditional safe haven (the Rothschilds and Pictets are well-known financial families), with exports and consumption strong. The Swiss National Bank capped the franc and the government imposed quotas on immigrants from eight central and eastern European nations. After tax-evasion scrutiny, it agreed to share more on nonresidents' accounts. It's still seen as safe for currency and gold.
- **The UK** grew 0.2% while most of Europe stagnated, and UHNW buyers are spending on luxury and real estate. London is a gateway; Middle Eastern or Asian families often open offices there. Global Partnership Family Offices has 2,000 members, over 70% from outside the UK, focused on investments, risk and efficiency more than family psychology.
- **Style:** European wealth management is business- and investment-oriented, conservative and slow-moving: research everything before acting. Old wealth is very private; no one asks about net worth. Much old wealth was lost in the world wars, so the modern family office is recent. The typical minimum is about €100 million; one Belgian business family's office manages about €1.9 billion; a large French family uses its office for investments, keeping the family together and philanthropy.
- **Steven Hoch** (Highmount Capital, U.S., Switzerland, Netherlands): many keep offices at home but bank through Switzerland, and the office is often still embedded in the business, seen as one total enterprise. Stewardship and keeping leadership in the bloodline are strong, with non-family executives and board members added. Southern European families are closer than northern ones; Nordic wealth is the most understated, German wealth more visible.
- **Ulrich Burkhard** (Marcuard Family Office, Switzerland): stay independent of banks to be unconflicted. European offices focus on multi-jurisdictional tax, accounting and compliance rather than securities, and Germany and the UK make direct investing hard; his office employs three lawyers. Since 2008 Europeans are even more careful about risk, and some SFOs use MFOs for one-off services.
- **Table 15.1** lists European MFOs from full-service to investment-only, consultancy, reporting and concierge. Examples: HQ Trust (Germany), set up to manage the Harald Quandt family's assets; Quilvest (Luxembourg), the Bemberg family office that branched into banking; Guggenheim Partners with operations in Geneva and London; IJ Partners, founded by Theodore Margellos, formerly of the World Economic Forum; Marcuard; Stanhope Capital (UK), with five founding families and a stake owned by Bessemer Trust; Iveagh, founded by a branch of the Guinness brewing dynasty; Sand Aire, founded by the Scott family; Capital Generation Partners, set up by Khaled Said, son of Wafic Said. Europe's MFO growth is perhaps the fastest outside the U.S.

**Middle East and Africa** (pp. 370-378). 8,075 UHNW people worth over $1.23 trillion. After the "Arab Spring," an economic renewal; Saudi Arabia and the UAE benefited most from exports, but Syria's war tempers optimism. The future needs economic diversification beyond limited oil and deeper financial markets. Dubai is the gateway like Hong Kong or Singapore; privacy is at a premium; families fly in advisors from New York, London, Geneva and Hong Kong. In Africa, oil exports, manufacturing, hotels and restaurants fuel new wealth and a growing middle class, with inflation a concern. Nigeria, South Africa and Egypt hold over two-thirds of Africa's UHNW population, but family office demand hasn't yet emerged meaningfully.
- **Wendy Craft:** families invest in what they know (a Saudi royal family member in a glass factory; Israeli families in New York property but not Kansas or Minnesota), with risk appetite returning since 2008.
- **Barbara Hauser:** Gulf family offices are informal. There are no tax-deduction concerns, so company employees often provide personal services. The Al-Zamil Group offers full family office services, including next-gen preparation, without a separate office. Separated offices are more professional. She estimates 30-40 in the Middle East (some based in London or Switzerland); much new Gulf wealth comes from investing; one Bahrain MFO, called simply The Family Office, mostly serves Arab families and is investment-focused. In Lebanon, attorney Hania Hammoud is championing the concept. Gulf families keep very close ties (marrying within tribes, cousins marrying) and multiple wives complicate who counts as "family." They are eager for best practices and prefer outside advisors for privacy.
- **Al-Touq Group:** a son helped his father invest; they added staff and an experienced CFO; another brother joined; formal reports go to the wider family. It is now professionally run with corporate governance, a website, direct investments in private firms internationally and occasional co-investing with friends.
- **Israel:** wealth is young apart from a few families. The Strauss family enterprise predates the state. High-tech exits over the past decade created wealth (Lyat Eyal, MMG Trust). Two types: a department inside a law or accounting firm acting like an MFO, and an accountant-run SFO or MFO, often a former corporate CFO recruited by the founder, a "one-man show."

**Oceania** (pp. 378-381). 3,955 UHNW people worth at least $485 billion. Australia's resource boom feeds China and India; Brisbane and Perth grow near coal and gas; Sydney is the international city; old wealth lives in Melbourne. Australia is vulnerable to China's slowdown and the euro crisis. New Zealand has mostly recovered from the 2011 Christchurch earthquake, with stocks up 24.3% (July 2012-June 2013). Wealth is mostly first and second generation, but Anglo-Saxon law and heritage bring strong trust and estate planning. Families are strategic, separate business from wealth, and worry about shirtsleeves to shirtsleeves. Richard Millroy (Private Wealth Network) estimates 300-400 family offices, about 200 established. With no wealth transfer taxes, planning has centered on investments, accounting and bookkeeping, and now extends to human capital, stewardship and governance. The Myer Family Office, a 1990s MFO built on stewardship and succession values, offers strategy, governance and philanthropy, competing with private bankers.

**The Gresen family case.** Fred Gresen emigrated from Europe just before WWII and opened a butcher shop in Melbourne. It grew into a major wholesale meat distributor, then diversified into plastics, glass, steel and recycling. By the 1990s grandson Anthony (a lawyer with business training and outside work in law and the family steel operations) saw that weekly family lunches couldn't run what it had become. The family formed a family advisory board, wrote a family constitution, hired a non-family CEO alongside Anthony as Managing Director, and added biannual shareholder meetings, a family council, a foundation and retreats every two to three years. Its challenges: 23 next-generation members aged 1-27 (what roles, if any?) and generational mathematics. Its answers: education, employment guidelines with outside-work requirements, and a next-gen board managing its own pool of funds.

**Global trends and best practices** (pp. 381-384). U.S. offices spend more time on accounting, reporting and compliance; international offices have more elaborate governance and more multi-jurisdictional planning. Culture and the source of wealth shape structure: old money is private and controlling; new money is more open and democratic. FOX and FBN's survey of 192 of the largest global family enterprises found all value preparing the next generation. Costs: the median office has six staff, and compensation can be up to 70% of the budget; complexity drives cost. Women are nearly 11% of UHNW, control over a quarter of assets under management (a third in North America), outlive spouses by seven to eight years, and are often poorly served by wealth managers (Joe Calabrese). An anti-wealth sentiment is rising: the U.S. Gini coefficient is 0.45, bringing scrutiny. The best practices:
1. **Cultivate the family:** a clear purpose for the wealth, regular formal and informal gatherings, open communication, shared identity through legacy and philanthropy.
2. **Cultivate stewardship:** engage the family in planning, attract top leaders and board members, transparent reporting, shareholder agreements, and conflict-resolution and buyout agreements for minority owners.
3. **Empower the next generation:** conduct expectations, employment policies, shared values, mentoring, and financial and lifestyle basics.
95% of family offices support philanthropy. Rockefeller (University of Chicago, Spelman College, national parks) and Carnegie (public libraries) show enduring legacies. Carnegie's dictum: spend the first third of life getting educated, the next making money, the last giving it away.

**Your 15-day arc.** You started with *what* a family office is and its three roles: keeper, guardian, brain trust (Ch 1). You learned *how families get there* (Ch 2), *which kind fits*, SFO or MFO (Ch 3), and that it all rests on values, mission and vision (Ch 4). Then you built it: legal structure and tax (Ch 5), the family baseline and capital sufficiency (Ch 6), SEC rules and compliance (Ch 7), investing for lifestyle and legacy (Ch 8), operations and technology (Ch 9), and talent (Ch 10). Then the human core: governance and succession (Ch 11), preparing heirs (Ch 12), the family bank and entrepreneurship (Ch 13), legacy and philanthropy (Ch 14), and finally how it all looks around the world (Ch 15). The thread from first page to last: **the money is the easy part; the family is the asset.** Structures exist to keep a family capable, connected and generous across generations.

3. Liliane Bettencourt, the L'Oréal heiress, was at the center of a French legal battle in the late 2000s after her daughter challenged huge gifts she had made to a photographer. Connect this case to the chapter's trend about women and wealth (longevity, control of assets, being poorly served by advisors) and to Chapter 6's idea of preparing for major life events like declining health.
